## The puzzle

Lessons 9–12 kept meeting the same pattern: a more flexible model (higher degree, smaller λ, smaller k) fits the training data better, yet past some point predicts new data worse. There are three regimes — **underfitting** (too simple to capture the truth), **good fit**, and **overfitting** (chasing noise). Why does extra flexibility eventually hurt? The answer is to stop thinking about the one model you fitted and imagine *all the models you could have fitted*.

## Imagine repeating the whole training process

Your training set is one random draw. Fix a query input $x$. Draw a fresh training set $D$, fit the same procedure, and record the prediction $\hat f_D(x)$. Repeat many times. Two different things can go wrong:

- **Bias** — the *average* prediction is in the wrong place: $\Bias(x) = \E_D[\hat f_D(x)] - f(x)$. A high-bias model pays little attention to the data and oversimplifies; it errs on training and test data alike.
- **Variance** — predictions scatter from one training set to the next: $\Var_D(\hat f_D(x)) = \E_D\big[(\hat f_D(x) - \E_D[\hat f_D(x)])^2\big]$. A high-variance model pays a lot of attention to its particular training data, does very well on it, and generalises poorly.

<Callout kind="warning">
The variance here is across *training sets* at a *fixed input*. It is not the spread of the raw y values, and not how much one model’s predictions vary across different x’s.
</Callout>

In these terms: **underfitting** typically means high bias and low variance; **overfitting** means the model has captured the noise along with the pattern — low bias, high variance.

## The decomposition

Assume $y = f(x) + \varepsilon$ with $\E[\varepsilon] = 0$, $\Var(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2$, and the test noise independent of the training data. Then the expected squared error of a new prediction at $x$ splits into three parts:

$$
\E\big[(y - \hat f(x))^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(f(x) - \E[\hat f(x)]\big)^2}_{\cbias{\Bias^2}} + \underbrace{\Var\big(\hat f(x)\big)}_{\cvar{\text{variance}}} + \underbrace{\Var(\varepsilon)}_{\cnoise{\text{irreducible error}}}.
$$

<Steps>
Derive it rather than memorise it. Write $a = \E_D[\hat f_D(x)]$ for the average prediction.

<Step title="Add and subtract the average prediction">
$$
y - \hat f_D = \underbrace{(f - a)}_{\text{fixed}} + \underbrace{(a - \hat f_D)}_{\text{mean } 0 \text{ over } D} + \underbrace{\varepsilon}_{\text{mean } 0}.
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Square and take expectations">
$$
\E[(y - \hat f_D)^2] = (f-a)^2 + \E[(a - \hat f_D)^2] + \E[\varepsilon^2] + 2\,\text{(cross terms)}.
$$
</Step>

<Step title="The cross terms vanish">
$$
\begin{aligned} 2(f-a)\,\E[a - \hat f_D] &= 0, \\ 2(f-a)\,\E[\varepsilon] &= 0, \\ 2\,\E[(a - \hat f_D)\varepsilon] &= 2\,\E[a - \hat f_D]\,\E[\varepsilon] = 0. \end{aligned}
$$

The last uses independence between the test noise and the training set.
</Step>

<Step title="Read off the three terms">
$$
\E[(y - \hat f_D(x))^2] = \Bias(x)^2 + \Var_D(\hat f_D(x)) + \sigma^2.
$$
</Step>
</Steps>

- **Irreducible error** $\sigma^2$ is the inherent noise in the system: unknown factors or chance. No model can remove it.
- **Reducible error** — bias² plus variance — can and should be reduced by adjusting the model.

<Callout kind="note">
If you measure error against the noiseless target $f(x)$ instead of a new noisy $y$, the $\sigma^2$ term disappears: $\E[(\hat f - f)^2] = \Bias^2 + \Var$. That two-term version is the MSE of an *estimator*.
</Callout>

## Make it concrete

Suppose $f(x) = 10$ and noise variance is 1. Procedure A’s predictions across training sets are 8, 10, 12 with equal probability: average 10, so bias 0; variance $(4 + 0 + 4)/3 = 8/3$; expected error $0 + 8/3 + 1 = 11/3 \approx 3.67$. Procedure B always predicts 9: bias −1, variance 0, expected error $1 + 0 + 1 = 2$.

**The biased procedure wins.** When comparing unbiased estimators we prefer the one with minimum variance, but in general we compare estimators with some bias and some variance, and a little bias can buy a lot less variance. That is exactly what regularisation does.

4. In the bottom chart, find the degree that minimises expected test error. Bias falls and variance rises with degree; their sum is U-shaped, sitting on the noise floor.
5. Move x₀ to the edges at high degree: variance is largest where data are sparse and the polynomial is least constrained.

## Finding the balance in practice

| To increase flexibility (↓ bias, ↑ variance) | To decrease flexibility (↑ bias, ↓ variance) |
| --- | --- |
| higher polynomial degree / more features | lower degree / fewer features |
| smaller ridge or LASSO λ | larger λ |
| smaller k in k-NN | larger k |

These are tendencies, not guaranteed monotone laws for every dataset. And since $f$ is unknown, there is no formula that tells you the balance point. Instead, estimate total error on **unseen validation data** and adjust the model’s complexity until it is minimised — precisely the protocol of lesson 11.

<Callout kind="intuition">
A common diagnostic: high training *and* validation error → likely high bias (underfit; add flexibility). Low training error but much higher validation error → likely high variance (overfit; regularise, simplify, or get more data). More data reduces variance; it does not fix bias from a too-simple model.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="key">
Imagine retraining on many datasets. Bias is how far the average prediction is from the truth; variance is how much predictions scatter between datasets.
