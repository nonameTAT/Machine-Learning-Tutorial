## Infinitely many lines — we need a referee

Draw a scatter plot and you can put infinitely many lines through it. Your eye prefers some of them, but “looks right” cannot be optimised. We need a single number that says how badly a candidate line fits, so that “best fit” becomes “smallest number”.

The natural building block is the **residual** — the error of the prediction at each training point:

$$
\cres{e_j} = y_j - \hat y_j = y_j - \bx_j\T\theta.
$$

A positive residual means the model *under*-predicts. Notice the direction we measure in: *vertically*, along the $y$ axis. That is deliberate. We are given $\bx_j$ and asked to predict $y_j$, so the error that matters is in $y$ at that fixed input. Ordinary least squares does **not** minimise perpendicular distances to the line.

## Why square the residuals?

The first idea — add up the residuals — fails immediately: positive and negative errors cancel. A terrible line with residuals $(-10, +10)$ has total 0. We need a penalty that is always non-negative. Two obvious candidates are $|e|$ and $e^2$.

Least squares chooses the square, for three reasons that will each get a lesson of their own:

- **It is smooth.** $e^2$ is differentiable everywhere, so we can set derivatives to zero (lessons 4–5) or follow the gradient (lesson 6). $|e|$ has a kink at 0.
- **It gives a closed-form answer.** The total squared error is a quadratic bowl in $\theta$ with a single bottom we can solve for exactly.
- **It has a probabilistic meaning.** With Gaussian noise, minimising squared error is the same as maximum likelihood (lesson 7).

The cost — or loss — function for $m$ observations is then the sum of squared errors:

$$
J(\theta) = \sum_{j=1}^{m}\Big(y_j - \sum_{i=0}^{n}\theta_i x_{ji}\Big)^2 = \sum_{j=1}^{m}(y_j - \bx_j\T\theta)^2 = (\by - X\theta)\T(\by - X\theta).
$$

In the OLS setting this is also called the **residual sum of squares** (RSS) or SSE. Dividing by $m$ gives the **mean squared error**:

$$
\MSE(\theta) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^m (y_j - \bx_j\T\theta)^2 = \frac{1}{m}\SSE(\theta).
$$

## Same minimiser, different minimum

Multiplying a function by a positive constant does not change *where* its minimum is, only *how deep* it is. So

$$
\argmin_\theta \SSE(\theta) = \argmin_\theta \MSE(\theta), \qquad \text{but}\qquad \min_\theta \MSE = \tfrac{1}{m}\min_\theta \SSE.
$$

This is the precise sense in which averaging leads to the same $\theta$. Keep the distinction between $\argmin$ (a parameter value) and $\min$ (an objective value) sharp: it matters for gradient descent, where scaling the loss scales the gradient and therefore the step size (lesson 6), and for regularisation, where it changes how strong a given $\lambda$ is (lesson 10).

## Beyond one feature: a plane of best fit

With two real-valued features $x_1, x_2$, the same criterion picks the “plane of best fit”: minimise the average squared *vertical* distance from each point to the plane. With more features it is a hyperplane. Nothing about the criterion changes — only the geometry of what is being fitted. Lesson 5 has a 3-D plane you can rotate.

## Why outliers matter

A residual of 10 contributes 100 to SSE; a residual of 1 contributes 1. Squared error therefore puts enormous weight on the few largest errors, and the fitted line bends towards an extreme point to reduce its square, even at the cost of slightly worse fits everywhere else.

This connects to a second property: at the least-squares fit (with an intercept), the residuals sum to zero,

$$
\sum_{j=1}^m (y_j - \bx_j\T\hat\theta) = 0.
$$

The line must “balance” the signed errors, so a single big positive residual must be offset by pulling the line upward. We will derive this identity in lesson 4 — it is the intercept’s normal equation.

<Callout kind="warning" title="Mechanism vs. symptom">
The *cause* of outlier sensitivity is the squared penalty: large errors dominate the objective. The zero-sum identity holds for any least-squares fit with a free intercept, outlier or not; it describes how the balance is struck, not why one point gets so much say. A loss like $|e|$ also balances errors but grows only linearly, so it is far less sensitive.
</Callout>

Points far from the centre of the $x$ range have extra leverage: moving them tilts the line around the centroid like a lever. You can feel this in the playground by dragging the end points versus the middle point.

<Callout kind="key">
“Best fit” needs a referee. Least squares uses the sum (or mean) of squared vertical residuals: non-negative, smooth, and solvable. SSE and MSE share a minimiser but not a minimum value. The square that makes the problem tractable is also what makes the fit sensitive to outliers.
</Callout>
