## Why switch to matrices?

With $n$ features the scalar approach needs $n+1$ partial derivatives that all look alike. Matrix notation does the same reasoning once for every coefficient: all predictions are $X\theta$, all residuals are $\be = \by - X\theta$, and the loss is a single squared length:

$$
\begin{gathered} J(\theta) = (\by - X\theta)\T(\by - X\theta) = \|\by - X\theta\|^2, \\[6pt] X = \begin{bmatrix}1 & x_{11} & \cdots & x_{1n}\\ 1 & x_{21} & \cdots & x_{2n}\\ \vdots & & \ddots & \vdots\\ 1 & x_{m1} & \cdots & x_{mn}\end{bmatrix},\qquad \by = \begin{bmatrix}y_1\\ y_2\\ \vdots\\ y_m\end{bmatrix}. \end{gathered}
$$

Two derivative rules are all you need: for a constant vector $\mathbf a$ and matrix $A$,

$$
\nabla_\theta(\mathbf a\T\theta) = \mathbf a, \qquad \nabla_\theta(\theta\T A\theta) = (A + A\T)\theta \;=\; 2A\theta \text{ if } A \text{ is symmetric}.
$$

## Deriving the normal equations

<Steps>
<Step title="Expand the quadratic">
$$
\begin{aligned} J(\theta) &= \by\T\by - \by\T X\theta - \theta\T X\T\by + \theta\T X\T X\theta \\ &= \by\T\by - 2\,\by\T X\theta + \theta\T X\T X\theta. \end{aligned}
$$

The two middle terms are equal because each is a $1\times1$ scalar, and a scalar equals its own transpose.
</Step>

<Step title="Differentiate term by term">
$$
\nabla J(\theta) = -2X\T\by + 2X\T X\theta = -2X\T(\by - X\theta).
$$

$X\T X$ is symmetric, so the quadratic rule gives $2X\T X\theta$.
</Step>

<Step title="Set the gradient to zero: the normal equations">
$$
X\T(\by - X\hat\theta) = \mathbf 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad X\T X\,\hat\theta = X\T\by.
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Solve, if X has full column rank">
$$
\boxed{\;\hat\theta = (X\T X)^{-1}X\T\by\;}
$$

Full column rank (no column is a linear combination of the others) is exactly what makes $X\T X$ invertible.
</Step>
</Steps>

With the MSE $\tfrac1m\|\by - X\theta\|^2$ instead, every gradient gains a factor $1/m$, giving $\nabla J = \tfrac2m X\T(X\theta - \by)$ — but the solution is identical, as lesson 2 predicted.

## Why the stationary point is the global minimum

A zero gradient alone could be a saddle. The second-derivative matrix (Hessian) settles it:

$$
H = \nabla^2 J(\theta) = 2X\T X, \qquad \bv\T H\bv = 2\,\bv\T X\T X\bv = 2\,\|X\bv\|^2 \;\ge\; 0 \quad\text{for every } \bv.
$$

So $H$ is positive semi-definite and $J$ is **convex**: every stationary point is a global minimum. If $X$ has full column rank, $X\bv \ne \mathbf 0$ whenever $\bv \ne \mathbf 0$, so $\bv\T H\bv > 0$: strictly convex, and the minimiser is unique.

<Callout kind="theorem" title="What this proves — and what it doesn’t">
Existence and uniqueness of the least-squares coefficients are *algebraic* facts about $X$. They say nothing about whether a linear model is appropriate, whether the sample is representative, or whether predictions will be accurate on new data. Those are the questions of lessons 8, 11 and 13.
</Callout>

## Geometry: least squares is a projection

Stack the $m$ targets into one vector $\by \in \R^m$, one axis per observation. Every possible prediction vector $X\theta = \theta_0\mathbf 1 + \theta_1\bx$ is a combination of the columns of $X$, so all of them lie in one flat subspace: the **column space** of $X$. Least squares picks the point of that subspace closest to $\by$, which is the foot of the perpendicular from $\by$. With three observations and two columns this can be drawn exactly: $\R^3$, and a plane through the origin.

<TryThis>
1. Rotate until you look edge-on along the plane: the residual $\be$ stands at a right angle to it, and the readout $X\T\be$ is $(0, 0)$ whatever $\by$ is.
2. Move $y_1$, $y_2$, $y_3$. $\hat\by$ slides around inside the plane but never leaves it, and $\be$ stays perpendicular.
3. Set $\by = (1, 2, 3)$ (or any $a + b\,x$). Now $\by$ lies in the plane, so $\be = \mathbf 0$ and the fit is exact.
4. Turn on “Compare with another θ”. The dashed $X\theta$ is another point of the plane, and $\by$, $\hat\by$, $X\theta$ form a right triangle, so $\|\by - X\theta\|^2 = \|\be\|^2 + \|X(\hat\theta - \theta)\|^2$: every other θ has a larger SSE.
</TryThis>

The normal equations $X\T\be = \mathbf 0$ say: the residual vector is **orthogonal to every column** of $X$. Read row by row, that is $\sum_j e_j = 0$ (ones column) and $\sum_j x_{ji}e_j = 0$ for every feature — the identities from lesson 4, now for any $n$. Intuitively: if the residual had any component along a feature direction, moving $\theta$ in that direction would shrink it, so we were not at the best fit.

<Callout kind="warning">
This orthogonality lives in $\R^m$ — the space with one axis per *observation*. It is not perpendicular distance on the x–y scatter plot. Least squares still minimises vertical distances there.
</Callout>

## Check: the one-feature case is a special case

For one feature,

$$
\begin{gathered} X\T X = \begin{bmatrix} m & \sum_j x_j\\ \sum_j x_j & \sum_j x_j^2\end{bmatrix}, \qquad X\T\by = \begin{bmatrix}\sum_j y_j\\ \sum_j x_jy_j\end{bmatrix}, \\[6pt] \det(X\T X) = m\sum_j x_j^2 - \Big(\sum_j x_j\Big)^2 = m\,S_{xx}. \end{gathered}
$$

So “$X\T X$ invertible” is exactly “$S_{xx} > 0$” from lesson 4. For the running example $x=(3,6,7,8,11)$, $y=(13,8,11,2,6)$:

$$
X\T X = \begin{bmatrix}5 & 35\\ 35 & 279\end{bmatrix},\qquad X\T\by = \begin{bmatrix}40\\ 246\end{bmatrix},\qquad \begin{bmatrix}5 & 35\\ 35 & 279\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}15\\ -1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}40\\ 246\end{bmatrix}. \;\checkmark
$$

## When the inverse does not exist

You need **full column rank**, not merely “lots of rows”. Each of these breaks it, regardless of $m$:

| Situation | Why columns are dependent | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Two identical (or proportional) columns | col₂ = c · col₁ | raise one coefficient, lower the other: same predictions |
| A feature that is constant across all rows | it is a multiple of the ones column | cannot separate its effect from the intercept |
| Height in cm and height in inches | proportional columns | coefficients not identifiable |
| More coefficients than rows (n + 1 > m) | rank ≤ m \< n + 1 | infinitely many exact fits |

A least-squares minimiser still exists in each case (the loss is still a convex bowl, now with a flat valley along the null-space direction), but it is not unique and $(X\T X)^{-1}$ is invalid. Ridge regression (lesson 10) restores uniqueness by adding $\lambda I$.

## A computing note

Forming $X\T X$ costs about $O(mn^2)$ and solving the $(n+1)\times(n+1)$ system $O(n^3)$. That is cheap for tens or hundreds of features and expensive for very many — one reason iterative methods like gradient descent (next lesson) exist. In practice, libraries solve the linear system (or use a QR decomposition) rather than computing an explicit inverse, which is slower and less accurate.
