## The problem: a better fit can be a worse model

Lesson 9 ended with a high-degree polynomial threading every training point and swinging wildly between them. Look at its coefficients and you find the mechanism: huge positive and negative weights that nearly cancel. Such a model is *extremely sensitive* — a small change in one data point produces a very different curve.

**Regularisation** is a general method to avoid overfitting by adding constraints to the weight vector. The common approach — **shrinkage** — insists the weights be small on average. We trade a little training fit for a lot less sensitivity. Note what it does *not* do: it adds no data. It changes the objective.

## Ridge regression: pay for large coefficients

Add a penalty to the least-squares cost:

$$
\begin{gathered} J_\lambda(\theta) = \underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^m (y_j - h_\theta(\bx_j))^2}_{\text{fit the data}} + \underbrace{\lambda\sum_{i=1}^n \theta_i^2}_{\text{keep weights small}}, \\[4pt] \theta^* = \argmin_\theta\,(\by - X\theta)\T(\by - X\theta) + \lambda\|\theta\|^2. \end{gathered}
$$

$\lambda \ge 0$ sets the exchange rate between fit and size. $\lambda = 0$ is ordinary least squares; $\lambda \to \infty$ drives the penalised coefficients to zero. Note the penalty sum starts at $i = 1$: the intercept is usually *not* penalised (shrinking it would just bias every prediction toward 0). Some texts write $\|\theta\|^2$ over all entries; both conventions appear, so always check which one a question uses.

<Steps>
The penalty is another quadratic, so the closed form survives. Let $D = \diag(0, 1, \dots, 1)$ so that $\theta\T D\theta = \sum_{i\ge1}\theta_i^2$.

<Step title="Write the objective in matrix form">
$$
J_\lambda(\theta) = (\by - X\theta)\T(\by - X\theta) + \lambda\,\theta\T D\theta.
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Differentiate (D is symmetric)">
$$
\nabla J_\lambda = -2X\T(\by - X\theta) + 2\lambda D\theta.
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Set to zero and collect θ">
$$
(X\T X + \lambda D)\,\hat\theta = X\T\by.
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Solve">
$$
\boxed{\;\hat\theta_{\text{ridge}} = (X\T X + \lambda D)^{-1}X\T\by\;}
$$

Penalising all entries replaces $D$ with $I$: $\hat\theta = (X\T X + \lambda I)^{-1}X\T\by$.

For $\lambda > 0$ this matrix is invertible even when $X\T X$ is not (duplicate columns, more features than rows): the penalty makes the bowl strictly convex in every penalised direction. Adding $\lambda$ to the diagonal is a classic trick for numerical stability.
</Step>
</Steps>

## See shrinkage in one dimension

With one feature and an unpenalised intercept, the ridge solution is

$$
\hat\theta_1 = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx} + \lambda}, \qquad \hat\theta_0 = \bar y - \hat\theta_1\bar x.
$$

The penalty simply inflates the denominator, pulling the slope toward 0. For the running example ($S_{xy} = -34, S_{xx} = 34$) with $\lambda = 34$: slope $-34/68 = -0.5$, intercept $8 - (-0.5)(7) = 11.5$.

|  | OLS (λ = 0) | Ridge (λ = 34) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fitted line | $15 - x$ | $11.5 - 0.5x$ |
| Training SSE | 40 | 48.5 |
| Penalised objective SSE + 34θ₁² | 40 + 34 = 74 | 48.5 + 8.5 = 57 |

Training SSE got *worse* — and that is exactly what the new objective asked for: it is willing to fit worse in exchange for a smaller slope. Whether this improves prediction on new data is a separate empirical question (lesson 11).

<Callout kind="note" title="SSE vs MSE convention changes λ’s meaning">
If the objective is $\MSE + \lambda\sum_{i\ge1}\theta_i^2$ instead, multiply through by $m$ and you get $(X\T X + m\lambda D)\hat\theta = X\T\by$. The same numerical λ is $m$ times stronger. (Above, λ = 34 under SSE equals λ = 6.8 under MSE with m = 5.)
</Callout>

## LASSO: absolute values buy sparsity

LASSO (“Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator”) replaces the squared penalty with absolute values:

$$
J_\lambda(\theta) = \|\by - X\theta\|^2 + \lambda\sum_{i=1}^n |\theta_i|.
$$

This small change has a big effect: some weights are shrunk, but others are set *exactly* to zero, so LASSO favours **sparse** solutions and performs feature selection as part of fitting.

## Why the L1 penalty produces exact zeros

A penalised problem has an equivalent constrained form: minimise SSE subject to $\sum\theta_i^2 \le t^2$ (ridge) or $\sum|\theta_i| \le t$ (LASSO). In two coordinates the ridge region is a disk; the LASSO region is a diamond with corners on the axes. The SSE contours are ellipses centred on the OLS solution $\hat\theta$. Grow the ellipses until they first touch the region: that contact point is the constrained solution.

<Lab title="Constraint geometry" purpose="Drag θ̂ (the OLS solution) in either panel. The orange ellipse is the first contour that touches each region.">
<PenaltyGeometryLab />
</Lab>

<TryThis>
1. Drag θ̂ around outside the regions. For the diamond, a whole range of positions makes contact at a corner — so a coordinate is exactly 0. For the disk, contact is almost always at a point with both coordinates non-zero.
2. Shrink t: more positions land on a corner (sparser LASSO solutions).
3. Change ρ to tilt the ellipses. Corners still attract contact, but which coefficient is zeroed can change.
</TryThis>

<Callout kind="note">
This explains a *tendency*: ridge can give a zero (if $\hat\theta$ lies on an axis), and LASSO need not (if the ellipse meets an edge). Also, the constraint size $t$ and the penalty $\lambda$ are related but not the same number.
</Callout>

## Practical points

- **Feature scale matters.** Changing a feature from metres to centimetres divides its coefficient by 100, so the same penalty treats it very differently. Standardise features before penalising — computing the means and scales on training data only.
- **λ is a hyperparameter.** Training error always prefers $\lambda = 0$, so choose λ with validation data or grid search (lesson 11).
- **Large-λ limit.** With an unpenalised intercept, every slope → 0 and the model predicts the training mean $\bar y$ everywhere.
- **Model selection by shrinkage**: one model whose unimportant variables get near-zero (ridge) or exactly zero (LASSO) coefficients — an alternative to searching over subsets.

<Callout kind="key">
