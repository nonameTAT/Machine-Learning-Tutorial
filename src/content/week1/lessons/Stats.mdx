## Why statistics shows up in machine learning

A training set is a *sample*. What we care about is how the model behaves on the whole *population* of inputs it will meet. Every claim about a fitted model — that a coefficient is meaningful, that test error predicts future error — is a claim from sample to population. This lesson gives the vocabulary for those claims and for the summaries (covariance, correlation) that the least-squares formulas are built from.

|  | Probability | Statistics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direction | population → samples | samples → population |
| Typical question | “If the coin is fair, how likely are 8 heads in 10?” | “Having seen 8 heads in 10, is the coin fair?” |
| Kind of reasoning | deductive — sound given the model | inductive — conclusions can be wrong |

## Sampling: when can a sample speak for the population?

You do not need to sip a cup of tea several times to decide that it is too hot: a homogeneous population needs few observations. Irregular populations need either a full census or a sample that *closely resembles* the population. What we want from a sampling method:

- no systematic bias — or none we cannot account for;
- the chance of an unrepresentative sample can be calculated (so we can refuse to conclude when it is high);
- that chance shrinks as the sample grows.

<Callout kind="warning" title="More data does not cure bias">
Larger samples reduce *random* sampling variation. They do nothing for a *systematic* flaw. A million house sales from only the most expensive suburbs is still a poor basis for predicting prices across the city — the estimate just becomes very precisely wrong.
</Callout>

## Estimators and what “unbiased” means

A **population parameter** (say the mean $\mu$) is a fixed but unknown number. An **estimator** is a rule applied to random data, such as $\bar x = \tfrac1N\sum_i x_i$. Because the sample is random, the estimate is random: draw another sample and you get another value.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Unbiased estimator">
$\hat\theta$ is unbiased for $\theta$ if $\E[\hat\theta] = \theta$: averaged over all the samples you could have drawn, it is exactly right. It says nothing about whether *your particular* estimate is close.
</Callout>

The standard estimators, with their bias status made precise:

| Estimator | Formula | Bias |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sample mean | $\bar x = \tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N x_i$ | unbiased for μ |
| Sample variance | $s^2 = \tfrac{1}{N-1}\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2$ | unbiased for σ² (i.i.d., finite variance) |
| Sample std. deviation | $s = \sqrt{s^2}$ | biased (usually low) — even though s² is unbiased |
| Sample median | middle ordered value | robust to outliers; unbiased for symmetric populations, generally biased otherwise |
| Sample range | $\max_i x_i - \min_i x_i$ | biased low: a sample rarely contains the population’s extremes |

**Why divide by N − 1?** The deviations are measured from $\bar x$, which is computed from the same data and is therefore closer to the data than the true $\mu$ is. Dividing by N would systematically under-estimate the spread. The derivation is short:

<Steps>
<Step title="Split each deviation through the true mean">
$$
\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2 = \sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2 - N(\bar x - \mu)^2
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Take expectations of each piece">
$$
\E\Big[\sum_i (x_i-\mu)^2\Big] = N\sigma^2, \qquad \E\big[(\bar x - \mu)^2\big] = \Var(\bar x) = \frac{\sigma^2}{N}.
$$

The second uses independence: the variance of an average of N i.i.d. variables is σ²/N.
</Step>

<Step title="Combine">
$$
\E\Big[\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2\Big] = N\sigma^2 - \sigma^2 = (N-1)\sigma^2 \;\Rightarrow\; \E[s^2] = \sigma^2.
$$

One “degree of freedom” is used up by estimating the mean.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Covariance: do the deviations move together?

Covariance asks a simple question of each pair: are $x_i$ and $y_i$ on the *same side* of their means? Same side gives a positive product $(x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)$, opposite sides a negative one. Averaging the products:

$$
\Cov(x,y) = \frac{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{N-1} = \frac{\big(\sum_i x_i y_i\big) - N\bar x\bar y}{N-1}.
$$

Its units are (units of x)·(units of y), so its size depends on measurement scale: switching from metres to centimetres multiplies it by 100. Correlation removes that scale.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Pearson correlation">
$$
r = \frac{\Cov(x,y)}{\sqrt{\Var(x)\,\Var(y)}} = \frac{\Cov(x,y)}{s_x s_y}, \qquad -1 \le r \le 1,
$$

defined when both standard deviations are positive. r near +1: high x with high y, low scatter; near 0: no *linear* association; near −1: strong inverse linear association.
</Callout>

You will meet this ratio again: the least-squares slope is $\hat\theta_1 = \Cov(x,y)/\Var(x) = r\,s_y/s_x$ (lesson 4). Same ingredients, different normalisation — slope carries units, r does not.

<Lab title="Covariance as signed areas" purpose="Each point and the mean point span a rectangle with area (xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ): blue when both deviations have the same sign, orange when they differ. Covariance adds the areas and divides by N − 1.">

<TryThis>

2. U-shape: the blue and orange areas cancel exactly, so the covariance and r are 0 — although y depends strongly on x. Covariance measures linear association only.
3. Measure x in centimetres: the picture and r do not change, but the covariance becomes 100 times larger.
4. Drag one point far from both means. Its rectangle can outweigh all the others put together.

## What correlation does and does not say

<Lab title="Correlation explorer" purpose="Generate datasets and watch how r, covariance and slope respond. Each mode targets one classic caution about correlation.">
<CorrelationLab />
</Lab>

- **It only measures linear association.** Take $x = (-1, 0, 1)$ and $y = x^2 = (1, 0, 1)$. Then $\bar x = 0$, and $\sum_i x_i (y_i - \bar y) = (-1)(1/3) + 0 + (1)(1/3) = 0$, so $r = 0$ despite a perfect relationship.
- **It does not model the relationship.** Given r and a new x you cannot compute y: you also need the means and standard deviations (that is exactly what the regression line adds).
- **Same correlation, different relationships** (Anscombe’s quartet) — and **same relationship, different correlation** (add more noise around the same line and r drops). So don’t compare datasets by their correlations.
- **It is not causation**, in either direction.

<Callout kind="warning">
“r = 0, so x is useless for predicting y” is false. The curve mode shows a near-perfect predictor with r ≈ 0. A zero correlation rules out only a linear trend.
</Callout>

<Quiz id="q1">
An estimator is unbiased. Which conclusion is justified?

<Option>Every estimate it produces equals the true parameter. <Why>Unbiasedness is about the average over repeated samples, not any single estimate.</Why></Option>
<Option correct>Its average over repeated samples equals the true parameter. <Why>That is the definition E[θ̂] = θ.</Why></Option>
<Option>It has the smallest possible error. <Why>An unbiased estimator can have large variance; a slightly biased one can have smaller total error (see lesson 13).</Why></Option>
</Quiz>

<Quiz id="q2">
Every value of y is doubled. What happens to Cov(x, y), r, and the least-squares slope?

<Option>All three double. <Why>r is scale-free, so it cannot double.</Why></Option>
<Option correct>Cov and slope double; r is unchanged. <Why>Cov(x, 2y) = 2Cov(x, y), slope = Cov/Var(x) doubles, while r divides by s_y which also doubles.</Why></Option>
<Option>Nothing changes. <Why>Covariance and slope carry the units of y.</Why></Option>
</Quiz>

<Quiz id="q3">
Why does the sample range tend to underestimate the population range?

<Option>Because it divides by N − 1. <Why>The range involves no division at all.</Why></Option>
<Option correct>Because the sample’s max and min can never be beyond the population’s, and usually fall short of them. <Why>max − min of a sample is always ≤ the population range, so its expectation is below it unless the extremes are certain to be sampled.</Why></Option>
<Option>It doesn’t; the range is unbiased. <Why>Try the simulation with the uniform population: the average sample range is below 10.</Why></Option>
</Quiz>

<Callout kind="key">
A sample is evidence about a population, and estimators are random. “Unbiased” means right on average across samples. Covariance measures whether deviations move together (in units); correlation rescales it to [−1, 1] and only detects *linear* association. Neither models the relationship, and neither establishes causation.
</Callout>
