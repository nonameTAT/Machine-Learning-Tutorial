<Problem id="A1" title="Loss scaling" lesson="loss">

Let $J(\theta) = \SSE(\theta)/m$ with $m > 1$. Compare the minimisers, the minimum values and the gradients of $J$ and SSE. Explain why the same gradient-descent learning rate is not equivalent for both.

<Hint>Multiply the entire loss by a positive constant: what changes and what doesn’t?</Hint>

<Solution>
Since $1/m > 0$, the ordering of candidate θ’s is unchanged, so the minimiser sets are identical. The minimum of $J$ is $1/m$ times the minimum SSE (equal only if both are 0). Also $\nabla J = \tfrac1m\nabla\SSE$, so a GD step on SSE with rate $\alpha$ is $m$ times longer than on $J$. To match a step on MSE with rate α, use rate $\alpha/m$ on SSE.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="A2" title="Derive, do not quote" lesson="ols1d">

For $J = \tfrac1m\sum_j (y_j - \theta_0 - \theta_1x_j)^2$, derive the two normal equations and then the slope and intercept. State the condition that makes the solution unique, and explain why the fitted line contains the centroid.

<Hint>Solve the intercept equation first; then substitute and use Σ(xⱼ − x̄) = 0.</Hint>

<Solution>
$$
\sum_j (y_j - \theta_0 - \theta_1x_j) = 0, \qquad \sum_j x_j(y_j - \theta_0 - \theta_1x_j) = 0.
$$

The first gives $\theta_0 = \bar y - \theta_1\bar x$. Substituting into the second gives $\theta_1S_{xx} = S_{xy}$, so $\hat\theta_1 = S_{xy}/S_{xx}$, $\hat\theta_0 = \bar y - \hat\theta_1\bar x$. Uniqueness requires $S_{xx} > 0$ (not all inputs equal). The prediction at $\bar x$ is $\hat\theta_0 + \hat\theta_1\bar x = \bar y$. Convexity makes the stationary point the minimum.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="A3" title="A zero gradient is not the whole proof" lesson="matrix">

Starting from $J = \|\by - X\theta\|^2/m$, obtain the matrix normal equations and show the stationary point is a global minimum. What extra condition makes it unique? What goes wrong with $(X\T X)^{-1}$ when two columns of $X$ are identical?

<Hint>Examine $\bv\T H\bv$ for the Hessian $H$.</Hint>

<Solution>
$\nabla J = \tfrac2m X\T(X\theta - \by) = 0 \Rightarrow X\T X\hat\theta = X\T\by$. The Hessian $H = \tfrac2m X\T X$ satisfies $\bv\T H\bv = \tfrac2m\|X\bv\|^2 \ge 0$, so $J$ is convex and every stationary point is a global minimiser. Full column rank gives $X\bv \ne 0$ for $\bv \ne 0$: strictly convex, unique minimiser. With duplicate columns, raising one coefficient and lowering the other by the same amount leaves $X\theta$ unchanged; $X\T X$ is singular and has no inverse.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="A4" title="Likelihood" lesson="mle">

With independent $y_j\mid\bx_j \sim \N(\bx_j\T\theta, \sigma^2)$ and fixed $\sigma^2 > 0$, derive the log-likelihood and explain why its maximiser is the OLS solution. Identify the exact step that uses independence.

<Hint>Take logs before differentiating.</Hint>

<Solution>
Independence gives $\Lik = \prod_j p(y_j\mid\bx_j;\theta)$ (this is the step). Taking logs of the Gaussian densities:

$$
\ell(\theta) = -\frac m2\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{\SSE(\theta)}{2\sigma^2}.
$$

The first term is constant in θ and the SSE coefficient is negative, so maximising ℓ is minimising SSE.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="A5" title="Regularisation convention" lesson="regularisation">

Derive the normal equations for the following objective, with the intercept unpenalised:

$$
J(\theta) = \frac1m\|\by - X\theta\|^2 + \lambda\sum_{i=1}^n\theta_i^2.
$$

State the penalty matrix and the coefficient multiplying it. What happens as λ → ∞?

<Hint>Multiply the gradient equation by m/2.</Hint>

<Solution>
With $D = \diag(0, 1, \dots, 1)$:

$$
0 = \frac2m X\T(X\theta - \by) + 2\lambda D\theta \;\Longrightarrow\; (X\T X + m\lambda D)\hat\theta = X\T\by.
$$

The coefficient is $m\lambda$ because the data term was averaged. As λ → ∞ all slopes → 0 and the unpenalised intercept → ȳ: a constant predictor.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="A6" title="Expected squared error" lesson="biasvar">

At fixed $x$, let $y = f(x) + \varepsilon$ and let $\hat f_D(x)$ depend on the training set. Derive the bias–variance decomposition. State the mean-zero and independence assumptions used, and explain why the noise term is absent from $\E_D[(\hat f_D(x) - f(x))^2]$.

<Hint>Add and subtract $\E_D[\hat f_D(x)]$ before squaring.</Hint>

<Solution>
Set $a = \E_D[\hat f_D(x)]$ and write $y - \hat f_D = (f - a) + (a - \hat f_D) + \varepsilon$. The first term is fixed, the second has mean zero over D, and ε has mean zero and is independent of D, so all cross terms have expectation zero. The squares give $\Bias^2 + \Var_D(\hat f_D) + \sigma^2$. Measuring against the fixed $f(x)$ instead of a noisy $y$ removes ε, leaving $\Bias^2 + \Var$.
</Solution>

</Problem>

</Group>

<Problem id="B1" title="A complete fit" lesson="ols1d">

Use $(x, y) = (0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 2)$. (a) Find the OLS slope, intercept, predictions and residuals. (b) Calculate SSE, MSE, RMSE, MAE and $R^2$. (c) Check both normal equations and predict at $x = 3$.

<Hint>Means first: x̄ = 1, ȳ = 5/3. Keep fractions.</Hint>

<Solution>
$S_{xx} = 2$, $S_{xy} = 1$, so $\hat\theta_1 = 1/2$, $\hat\theta_0 = 5/3 - 1/2 = 7/6$. Predictions $(7/6, 5/3, 13/6)$, residuals $(-1/6, 1/3, -1/6)$.

$$
\begin{gathered} \SSE = \tfrac16,\quad \MSE = \tfrac1{18},\quad \RMSE = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{18}} \approx 0.236, \\ \MAE = \tfrac29,\quad \SST = \tfrac23,\quad R^2 = 1 - \tfrac{1/6}{2/3} = \tfrac34. \end{gathered}
$$

$\sum_j e_j = -\tfrac16 + \tfrac13 - \tfrac16 = 0$ and $\sum_j x_je_j = 0(-\tfrac16) + 1(\tfrac13) + 2(-\tfrac16) = 0$. At $x = 3$: $7/6 + 3/2 = 8/3$, an extrapolation beyond the observed inputs. Check it with the [OLS calculator](#/ols1d).
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="B2" title="One gradient step" lesson="gd">

For B1’s data, start at $\theta = (0, 0)\T$ with $\alpha = 0.1$. Using MSE, perform one simultaneous batch update. Separately, perform one single-example LMS update using only $(0, 1)$. Why are the results different?

<Solution>
$$
\begin{gathered} \be = (1, 2, 2)\T,\quad X\T\be = (5, 6)\T,\quad \nabla J = -\tfrac23(5, 6)\T = (-\tfrac{10}3, -4)\T \\ \Rightarrow\; \theta^{(1)} = (0, 0)\T - 0.1\,(-\tfrac{10}3, -4)\T = (\tfrac13, \tfrac25)\T. \end{gathered}
$$

LMS on (0, 1): $\theta + 2(0.1)(1)(1, 0)\T = (0.2, 0)\T$. Batch averages all three examples’ gradients; LMS uses only the first. Both use the original θ for every component.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="B3" title="Ridge by hand" lesson="regularisation">

For B1, minimise $\SSE + 2\theta_1^2$ with an unpenalised intercept. Find the coefficients. What penalty in $\MSE + \lambda\theta_1^2$ gives the same solution?

<Solution>
Slope $S_{xy}/(S_{xx} + 2) = 1/4$, intercept $5/3 - 1/4 = 17/12$. With $m = 3$, $\MSE + \lambda\theta_1^2$ times 3 is $\SSE + 3\lambda\theta_1^2$, so $\lambda = 2/3$. Residuals $(-5/12, 1/3, 1/12)$ still sum to zero (free intercept); their SSE is $7/24 > 1/6$, an increase the penalised objective accepts.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="B4" title="Interactions" lesson="features">

Consider $\hat y = 10 + 2D + 3x - 0.5Dx$, $D \in \{0, 1\}$. Write the rule for each group and the predicted group difference at $x$. Where do the predictions coincide? Is the model linear in its parameters?

<Solution>
$D=0: 10 + 3x$; $D=1: 12 + 2.5x$. Difference $2 - 0.5x$, zero at $x = 4$. Yes: the features $(1, D, x, Dx)$ are fixed columns and the prediction is a linear combination of them.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="B5" title="Local predictions" lesson="knn">

Store $(0,1), (2,3), (5,7), (8,9)$ and query $x_q = 3$. Find the 1-NN and 2-NN regression predictions. Then fit a local line through the two nearest observations and predict at 3.

<Solution>
Distances 3, 1, 2, 5. 1-NN: (2, 3) → 3. 2-NN: (2, 3) and (5, 7) → 5. The local line through them has slope 4/3 and intercept 1/3, predicting $1/3 + 4 = 13/3 \approx 4.33$. Averaging assumes a flat neighbourhood; the local line follows its trend.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="B6" title="Correlation, slope and R²" lesson="stats">

For B1’s data compute $s_x, s_y, \Cov(x, y)$ and Pearson $r$. Verify that $\hat\theta_1 = r\,s_y/s_x$ and that $R^2 = r^2$.

<Hint>Use the N − 1 denominators: s²ₓ = S_xx/2, s²_y = SST/2, Cov = S_xy/2.</Hint>

<Solution>
$s_x^2 = 1$, $s_y^2 = (2/3)/2 = 1/3$, $\Cov = 1/2$, so $r = \frac{1/2}{1\cdot\sqrt{1/3}} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2} \approx 0.866$. Then $r s_y/s_x = (\sqrt3/2)(1/\sqrt3) = 1/2 = \hat\theta_1$ ✓ and $r^2 = 3/4 = R^2$ ✓. (For one-feature OLS with an intercept, training R² always equals r².)
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="B7" title="Adjusted R²" lesson="evaluation">

On $m = 20$ training examples, model P has 3 predictors and $R^2 = 0.80$; model Q has 8 predictors and $R^2 = 0.82$. Compare their adjusted R². What else would you want before choosing?

<Solution>
P: $1 - 0.20\cdot\tfrac{19}{16} = 0.7625$. Q: $1 - 0.18\cdot\tfrac{19}{11} \approx 0.689$. Adjusted R² prefers P: Q’s small gain in fit does not justify five extra predictors. Better still, compare validation error — adjusted R² is still a training-data quantity.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Group id="C" title="C · Diagnose the claim" intro="True or false — and give the mechanism or a counterexample, not just a verdict.">

<Problem id="C1" title="Diagnose the claim" lesson="loss">

“The training residuals sum to zero, so the predictions are accurate.”

<Solution>
**False.** Signed residuals can cancel even when errors are large: (−10, 10) sums to 0 with MSE 100. Σe = 0 is the intercept’s normal equation, not an accuracy certificate.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="C2" title="Diagnose the claim" lesson="matrix">

“An intercept plus two identical feature columns must produce a unique OLS coefficient vector as long as there are 1,000 observations.”

<Solution>
**False.** Identical columns are linearly dependent whatever the number of rows. Shifting weight between them leaves predictions unchanged, so the coefficients are not unique and $(X\T X)^{-1}$ does not exist.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="C3" title="Diagnose the claim" lesson="evaluation">

“A model with negative test R² was necessarily calculated incorrectly.”

<Solution>
**False.** Test R² \< 0 means the model’s test SSE exceeds that of predicting the test mean. It happens when a model generalises poorly.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="C4" title="Diagnose the claim" lesson="evaluation">

“I selected λ by trying 30 values and reporting whichever had the lowest test MSE. Because no coefficients were fitted on the test set, the reported score is a fair final evaluation.”

<Solution>
**False.** The test set influenced the choice of λ, so it was used for model selection. Tune on validation data and keep an untouched test set for the single final evaluation; the reported score here is optimistic.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="C5" title="Diagnose the claim" lesson="stats">

“Pearson correlation is zero, so the input cannot predict the output.”

<Solution>
**False.** $y = x^2$ on $x = (-1, 0, 1)$ has r = 0 and is perfectly predictable. r only measures linear association.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="C6" title="Diagnose the claim" lesson="biasvar">

“The mean of a model’s predictions over inputs is correct, so the model has zero bias in the bias–variance decomposition.”

<Solution>
**False.** Bias compares the expected prediction *across training sets at a fixed input* with f(x). Averaging over different inputs is a different operation, and errors of opposite sign at different x can cancel.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="C7" title="Diagnose the claim" lesson="gd">

“Gradient descent diverged on my least-squares problem, so the loss must not be convex.”

<Solution>
**False.** The MSE is always convex. Divergence with a fixed step means α exceeded the stability limit $1/\lambda_{\max}(X\T X/m)$; reduce α or rescale the features.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="C8" title="Diagnose the claim" lesson="assumptions">

“House prices are right-skewed, so the normality assumption of linear regression is violated.”

<Solution>
**Not necessarily.** The assumption concerns y *given* x (the noise around the mean), not the overall distribution of y. A skewed marginal can arise from a skewed distribution of features.
</Solution>

</Problem>

</Group>

<Problem id="D1" title="Choose and explain" lesson="biasvar">

At a fixed input, procedure A predicts 4 or 8 with equal probability over training sets; procedure B always predicts 5. The true mean response is 6 and independent test-noise variance is 2. Compute each procedure’s bias, variance and expected squared prediction error. Which is preferable, and what does this show about unbiasedness?

<Solution>
A: mean 6, bias 0, variance $((4-6)^2 + (8-6)^2)/2 = 4$, error $0 + 4 + 2 = 6$. B: bias −1, variance 0, error $1 + 0 + 2 = 3$. B is preferable under squared loss: lower variance can more than compensate for some bias. Both face the same irreducible noise.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="D2" title="Choose and explain" lesson="regularisation">

You have 60 examples and 200 candidate features, and you believe only a few matter. Explain why plain least squares is problematic, and which of ridge or LASSO you would try first, and how you would choose λ.

<Solution>
With $n + 1 = 201 > m = 60$, $X$ cannot have full column rank: OLS has infinitely many exact-fit solutions and will overfit. Both penalties restore a unique, stable solution; LASSO is the natural first choice because its L1 penalty yields exact zeros, selecting a sparse subset. Standardise the features using training statistics, choose λ by validation or cross-validation error, and report test error once.
</Solution>

</Problem>
