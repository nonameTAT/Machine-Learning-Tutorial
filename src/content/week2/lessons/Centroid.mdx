## A line splits the plane into two sides

The simplest discriminative idea: draw a line between the two clouds of fish and classify by which side a new fish falls on. The line $ax_1 + bx_2 + c = 0$ can be oriented so that $ax_1 + bx_2 + c > 0$ above it and $< 0$ below it. Collect the coefficients into a **weight vector** $\bw = (a, b)\T$ and write $t = -c$:

$$
\text{boundary: } \bx\T\bw = t, \qquad \hat y = \begin{cases} 1 & \bx\T\bw > t \\ 0 & \bx\T\bw < t. \end{cases}
$$

The quantity $\bx\T\bw$ is a *score* and $t$ is the **decision threshold**. Points exactly on the boundary need a stated tie rule (this site sends ties to the positive class). In $d$ dimensions the same equation describes a hyperplane, as long as $\bw \neq \mathbf{0}$.

<Callout kind="theorem" title="Why w is perpendicular to the boundary">
Take any two points on the boundary: $\bx_a\T\bw = t$ and $\bx_b\T\bw = t$. Subtracting, $(\bx_a - \bx_b)\T\bw = 0$. Every direction *along* the boundary is orthogonal to $\bw$. Moving in the direction of $\bw$ increases the score, so $\bw$ points towards the positive side.
</Callout>

## The basic linear classifier

Which line? The **basic linear classifier** makes the most naive choice that still uses the data: compute the centre of mass of each class — $\bp$ for the positives and $\bn$ for the negatives — and cut the segment between them in half with a perpendicular line.

<Steps>
Two facts pin the line down: it is perpendicular to the segment from n to p, and it passes through the midpoint.

<Step title="Perpendicular to p − n">
The normal vector of the boundary is $\bw$, so take $\bw = \bp - \bn$. It points from the negative mean to the positive mean, i.e. towards the positive side, as required.
</Step>

<Step title="The midpoint lies on the boundary">
$$
\ba = \frac{\bp + \bn}{2}, \qquad t = \ba\T\bw = \frac{(\bp + \bn)\T(\bp - \bn)}{2}.
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Expand — the cross terms cancel">
$$
(\bp + \bn)\T(\bp - \bn) = \bp\T\bp - \bp\T\bn + \bn\T\bp - \bn\T\bn = \|\bp\|^2 - \|\bn\|^2,
$$

because $\bp\T\bn = \bn\T\bp$. Hence

$$
\boxed{\;\bw = \bp - \bn, \qquad t = \frac{\|\bp\|^2 - \|\bn\|^2}{2}\;}
$$
</Step>
</Steps>

<Callout kind="note">
“Training” is just computing two means. The construction does *not* search for the best separating line: it ignores the spread and shape of each class, and can misclassify training points even when a perfect separator exists.
</Callout>

## The same classifier, seen as nearest centroid

Lesson 13 rediscovers this classifier from a distance-based angle: classify a query as positive if it is closer to $\bp$ than to $\bn$. Under Euclidean distance these are *the same rule*.

<Steps>
<Step title="Compare squared distances">
Distances are non-negative, so squaring preserves the comparison:

$$
\|\bx - \bp\|^2 < \|\bx - \bn\|^2.
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Expand both sides">
$$
\bx\T\bx - 2\bx\T\bp + \|\bp\|^2 < \bx\T\bx - 2\bx\T\bn + \|\bn\|^2.
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Cancel xᵀx and rearrange">
$$
2\bx\T(\bp - \bn) > \|\bp\|^2 - \|\bn\|^2 \iff \bx\T\bw > t.
$$

The quadratic terms cancel, which is why the boundary is linear. Exactly the basic linear classifier: the boundary is the set of points equidistant from the two means.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Callout kind="example" title="Worked example">
Positives $(3,3), (5,3)$ give $\bp = (4, 3)$; negatives $(1,0), (1,2)$ give $\bn = (1, 1)$. Then $\bw = (3, 2)$ and $t = (25 - 2)/2 = 11.5$, so the boundary is $3x_1 + 2x_2 = 11.5$. The query $(2, 3)$ scores $6 + 6 = 12 > 11.5$: positive. Check: its squared distances are $4 + 0 = 4$ to $\bp$ and $1 + 4 = 5$ to $\bn$. The midpoint $(2.5, 2)$ scores exactly 11.5.
</Callout>

## When one mean per class is not enough

- **Coinciding means.** If $\bp = \bn$, then $\bw = \mathbf{0}$ and $t = 0$: every query ties. The classes may still be completely different — a ring around a disc, say — but a single mean cannot see it.
- **Multimodal classes**. A class made of several clumps is badly summarised by one point. One remedy is several exemplars per class (lesson 13).
- **Outliers** pull a mean, and therefore the whole boundary.

<Callout kind="key">
A linear classifier predicts by the sign of $\bx\T\bw - t$; $\bw$ is normal to the boundary and points to the positive side. The basic linear classifier takes $\bw = \bp - \bn$ and $t = (\|\bp\|^2 - \|\bn\|^2)/2$, which is exactly “nearest class mean” under Euclidean distance — simple and fast, but blind to anything a mean cannot capture.
