## An exemplar summarises a group

An **exemplar** is a point used to represent a larger group of data. In distance-based learning, a natural way to choose one is to make it close to every member — but “close” depends on the distance, and on whether the exemplar must be one of the data points. The mean, the geometric median and the medoid answer three different questions.

## The mean minimises the sum of squared distances

The arithmetic mean is the *unique* point minimising the sum of squared Euclidean distances to the data. Here is why, for points $\bx_1, \dots, \bx_m$ with mean $\bmu = \frac1m\sum_j\bx_j$ and any candidate centre $\mathbf c$.

<Steps>
<Step title="Insert the mean">
$$
\bx_j - \mathbf c = (\bx_j - \bmu) + (\bmu - \mathbf c).
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Expand the squared norm and sum">
$$
\sum_j\|\bx_j - \mathbf c\|^2 = \sum_j\|\bx_j - \bmu\|^2 + 2(\bmu - \mathbf c)\T\sum_j(\bx_j - \bmu) + m\|\bmu - \mathbf c\|^2.
$$
</Step>

<Step title="The cross term vanishes">
Deviations from the mean sum to zero: $\sum_j(\bx_j - \bmu) = \mathbf 0$. So

$$
\boxed{\;\sum_j\|\bx_j - \mathbf c\|^2 = \sum_j\|\bx_j - \bmu\|^2 + m\|\mathbf c - \bmu\|^2\;}
$$

The first term does not depend on $\mathbf c$; the second is ≥ 0 and zero only at $\mathbf c = \bmu$. The mean is the unique minimiser. (This is the vector version of Week 1’s result that the best constant predictor under squared error is $\bar y$.)
</Step>
</Steps>

## Change the objective, or the allowed centres

| Exemplar | Minimises | Must it be a data point? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mean (centroid) | sum of squared Euclidean distances | no |
| Geometric median | sum of (unsquared) Euclidean distances | no |
| Medoid | total distance to the others, under the chosen distance | yes — and there may be more than one |

<Callout kind="example" title="Points 0, 2, 10">
The mean is 4 — not an observed point. Candidate medoids under absolute distance:

| candidate | total distance | value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 0 + 2 + 10 | 12 |
| 2 | 2 + 0 + 8 | 10 |
| 10 | 10 + 8 + 0 | 18 |

The medoid is 2 (and here the geometric median is also 2).
</Callout>

Finding a medoid means computing, for each data point, its total distance to all others — $O(n^2)$ distance evaluations for $n$ points whatever the metric, so there is no *computational* reason to prefer one metric for medoids. (Each distance evaluation still costs time proportional to the number of features.)

## The nearest-centroid classifier

Use one exemplar per class — its centroid — and classify by the minimum-distance principle:

$$
\bmu_c = \frac{1}{|C_c|}\sum_{j\in C_c}\bx_j, \qquad \hat y = \argmin_c\, d(\bx, \bmu_c), \qquad C_c = \{j : y_j = c\}.
$$

With two classes and Euclidean distance this is exactly the basic linear classifier from lesson 2: it constructs exemplars that minimise squared Euclidean distance within each class, then applies a nearest-exemplar rule.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| simple | poor for complex classes: multimodal or non-spherical |
| fast — one distance per class at prediction time | cannot handle outliers and noisy data well (they move the mean) |
| works well when classes are compact and far apart | cannot handle missing data |

**What if a class has more than one mode?**. One centroid per class lands between the modes and performs poorly. If we can find the modes, one centroid per mode helps — finding such groups is clustering, a later topic.

<Callout kind="key">
An exemplar should match its objective: the mean uniquely minimises squared Euclidean distance (the cross term vanishes), the geometric median minimises unsquared distance, and the medoid is the best *observed* point (an $O(n^2)$ search). Nearest centroid classifies by the closest class mean — the basic linear classifier again — and fails when a mean cannot represent its class.
</Callout>
