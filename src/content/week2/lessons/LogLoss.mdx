## Which coefficients? Ask which make the labels most probable

Lesson 3 gave a model with unknown coefficients β. To fit it we need an objective. Week 1 found one with **maximum likelihood**: tell a story of how the data arise, then choose the parameters under which the observed data are most probable. With Gaussian noise that story produced squared error. Here the response is a single yes/no outcome, so the natural story is a biased coin whose probability of heads depends on the input.

Write $p_j = h_\beta(\bx_j) = \sigma(\bxt_j\T\beta)$. The probability the model assigns to the label actually observed can be written in one line:

$$
P(Y_j = y_j \mid \bx_j; \beta) = p_j^{\,y_j}(1 - p_j)^{1 - y_j}.
$$

Check both cases: if $y_j = 1$ the second factor is $(1-p_j)^0 = 1$, leaving $p_j$; if $y_j = 0$ the first factor is 1, leaving $1 - p_j$. The exponents act as a switch that picks the probability of the true label. This is a **Bernoulli** model whose parameter depends on the input.

## From the likelihood to the loss

<Steps>
Assume the observations are independent given their inputs. Predict each line before revealing it.

<Step title="Independence: the likelihood is a product">
$$
\Lik(\beta) = \prod_{j=1}^m P(Y_j = y_j \mid \bx_j; \beta) = \prod_{j=1}^m p_j^{\,y_j}(1 - p_j)^{1 - y_j}.
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Take logs: products become sums">
$$
\ell(\beta) = \log\Lik(\beta) = \sum_{j=1}^m \big[y_j\log p_j + (1 - y_j)\log(1 - p_j)\big].
$$

Log is strictly increasing, so it does not move the maximiser. It also avoids multiplying hundreds of numbers below 1.
</Step>

<Step title="Negate and average: a loss to minimise">
$$
\boxed{\;J(\beta) = -\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m \big[y_j\log p_j + (1 - y_j)\log(1 - p_j)\big]\;}
$$

This is the cost function, usually called **binary cross-entropy** or **log loss** — two names for the same formula. Maximising ℓ and minimising $J$ give the same β; the $1/m$ only rescales. Logs are natural logs.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Callout kind="note" title="Where each assumption is used">
**Independence** is used once: to write the joint probability as a product. The **binary labels** are used to select the right factor through the exponents $y_j$ and $1 - y_j$. The sigmoid form of $p_j$ has not been used yet — it enters when we differentiate (lesson 5).
</Callout>

## Read the loss one observation at a time

For a positive observation the loss is $-\log p$; for a negative one it is $-\log(1 - p)$. Either way, the model pays $-\log(\text{probability it gave to the true label})$.

| true y | predicted p | loss | interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0.9 | $-\log 0.9 \approx 0.105$ | confident and correct |
| 1 | 0.1 | $-\log 0.1 \approx 2.303$ | confident and wrong |
| 0 | 0.1 | $-\log 0.9 \approx 0.105$ | high probability on class 0 — correct |
| 0 | 0.9 | $-\log 0.1 \approx 2.303$ | low probability on the true class |

Both $p = 0.51$ and $p = 0.99$ predict class 1 at threshold ½, but their losses for a positive are 0.673 and 0.010. Accuracy cannot tell them apart; likelihood-based training can. And as the probability of the true label tends to 0, its loss tends to infinity.

## Why not reuse squared error?

Plugging the sigmoid into the Week 1 squared loss gives a **non-convex** function of the coefficients, with flat regions where gradient methods make little progress. Log loss, by contrast, is convex (lesson 5).

<Lab title="Both losses as a function of one coefficient" purpose="Eight points, two of them on the ‘wrong’ side; score z = b·x. Move b and compare the shapes.">

</Lab>

<TryThis>
1. Move b far to the left. Log loss keeps rising (it punishes the confident mistakes); squared error levels off at 0.75 — the gradient almost vanishes there.
2. Compare the two minimisers. They need not coincide: the objectives are different, not two ways of writing the same one.
3. Explain, without calculus, why a bounded function that is not constant cannot be convex on the whole real line.
</TryThis>

<Callout kind="warning" title="Keep the claim precise">
Squared error of a sigmoid can be minimised — it is not “impossible”. It just lacks the likelihood justification and the convexity that make log loss the natural choice. Conversely, binary labels do not make every least-squares calculation invalid; the model, objective and interpretation must simply match.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="key">
A binary label given $\bx$ is Bernoulli with probability $p = \sigma(\bxt\T\beta)$, so $P(y \mid \bx) = p^y(1 - p)^{1-y}$. Independence makes the likelihood a product; its negative average log is the log loss $J(\beta) = -\frac1m\sum_j[y_j\log p_j + (1 - y_j)\log(1 - p_j)]$, which charges $-\log$ of the probability given to the true label.
</Callout>
