## Why not just fit a line to the 0/1 labels?

Plot a binary label against one input and you get two horizontal rows of points. Week 1 gives us a hammer — least squares — so why not fit a line and threshold it at ½? There are several problems. The decisive one is about *what the output means*:

- We want $P(y = 1 \mid \bx)$, a number in $[0, 1]$. A line is unbounded: far enough out it predicts values below 0 or above 1, which cannot be probabilities.
- The Week 1 justification for squared error was Gaussian noise around the mean (lesson 7 of Week 1). A binary response is not Gaussian, so that likelihood argument does not apply. (Least-squares *algebra* still runs — it just lacks the probabilistic justification.)
- A line also reacts to points far from the boundary: an easy, very large x drags the line and shifts where it crosses ½.

The fix keeps the linear score but changes the *output scale*: feed the score through a function that squashes every real number into (0, 1).

## The sigmoid turns a score into a probability

Logistic regression models

$$
P(y = 1 \mid \bx) = h_\beta(\bx) = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}, \qquad z = \bxt\T\beta = \beta_0 + \sum_{r=1}^d \beta_rx_r,
$$

$$
P(y = 0 \mid \bx) = 1 - h_\beta(\bx).
$$

The labels stay 0 or 1; the model estimates their *conditional probabilities*. Logistic regression seeks to model the probability of a class given the inputs, estimate it for a new observation, and classify from that estimate.

| Property of σ | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| $0 < \sigma(z) < 1$ | every finite score gives a valid probability |
| $\sigma(0) = \tfrac12$ | score 0 means “undecided” |
| strictly increasing | a larger score always means a larger probability: the ordering of scores is preserved |
| $\sigma(-z) = 1 - \sigma(z)$ | the two classes are treated symmetrically |
| $\sigma(z) \to 1$ as $z \to \infty$ | probabilities approach, but never reach, 0 and 1 |

<Callout kind="example">
With $z = -2 + x$: at $x = 1, 2, 4$ the scores are $-1, 0, 2$ and the probabilities are $\sigma(-1) \approx 0.269$, $0.5$, $\sigma(2) \approx 0.881$.
</Callout>

## The decision boundary is still linear

Predict class 1 when the estimated probability is at least ½. Because σ is increasing and σ(0) = ½:

$$
h_\beta(\bx) \ge 0.5 \iff \bxt\T\beta \ge 0.
$$

The *probability* is a nonlinear function of the score, but the *decision boundary* $\bxt\T\beta = 0$ is a hyperplane in feature space. That is why logistic regression is called a linear model. As in Week 1, adding transformed features (say $x^2$) gives a boundary that is linear in the new features and curved in the original input.

<Steps>
The ½ threshold is a convention, not a law. For a general threshold τ, invert the sigmoid.

<Step title="Start from the probability condition">
$$
\sigma(z) \ge \tau \iff \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} \ge \tau, \qquad 0 < \tau < 1.
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Rearrange (all quantities positive)">
$$
1 + e^{-z} \le \frac1\tau \iff e^{-z} \le \frac{1 - \tau}{\tau}.
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Take logs (log is increasing)">
$$
\boxed{\;\sigma(z) \ge \tau \iff z \ge \log\frac{\tau}{1 - \tau}\;}
$$

$\log\frac{\tau}{1 - \tau}$ is the *log-odds* (logit) of τ. For τ = 0.5 it is 0, recovering the rule above.
</Step>

<Step title="Apply it to z = −2 + x with τ = 0.8">
$\log(0.8/0.2) = \log 4 \approx 1.386$, so predict positive when $x \ge 2 + \log 4 \approx 3.386$. Raising the threshold moves the boundary *without refitting* the probability model — the mechanism behind ROC curves (lesson 9).
</Step>
</Steps>

## Reading the coefficients

Because $\log\frac{p}{1 - p} = \bxt\T\beta$, logistic regression is linear in the **log-odds**. Increasing feature $x_r$ by one unit (others fixed) adds $\beta_r$ to the log-odds, i.e. multiplies the odds $p/(1 - p)$ by $e^{\beta_r}$. This is one sense in which the model is “easy to interpret” — but note it is the *odds*, not the probability, that change by a constant factor.

<Callout kind="warning">
Multiplying every coefficient by a positive constant $c$ leaves the sign of the score — and so the ½-boundary and the labels away from ties — unchanged. It does *not* leave the probabilities unchanged: $\sigma(cz)$ is more extreme than $\sigma(z)$ for $c > 1$. The labels and the probabilities are different outputs.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="key">
Logistic regression keeps a linear score $z = \bxt\T\beta$ and squashes it with $\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})$ into an estimate of $P(y = 1 \mid \bx)$. Since σ is increasing, $\sigma(z) \ge \tau \iff z \ge \log\frac{\tau}{1 - \tau}$: the boundary is a hyperplane, and changing τ moves it without refitting.
</Callout>
