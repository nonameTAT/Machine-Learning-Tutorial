<Problem id="A1" title="Generative classification" lesson="classifier">

For an input $\bx$, $p(\bx \mid Y=1) = 0.4$, $p(\bx \mid Y=0) = 0.1$, and the priors are $P(Y=1) = 0.1$, $P(Y=0) = 0.9$. Compute the posterior probability of class 1 and predict under the most-probable-class rule. Explain why the larger likelihood alone is not decisive.

<Hint>Compare likelihood × prior.</Hint>

<Solution>
Products: $0.4 \times 0.1 = 0.04$ and $0.1 \times 0.9 = 0.09$. So $P(Y=1 \mid \bx) = 0.04/0.13 = 4/13 \approx 0.308$: predict class 0. Its lower feature likelihood is outweighed by its much larger prior. The normalising denominator $p(\bx) = 0.13$ is shared by both classes, so comparing the numerators is enough.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="A2" title="From means to a boundary" lesson="centroid">

The class centroids are $\bp = (3, 2)$ and $\bn = (-1, 0)$. Derive $\bw$ and $t$ for the basic linear classifier. Check that the midpoint lies on the boundary, classify $(1, 2)$, and verify by comparing squared distances. Then reconstruct the general argument that “equal Euclidean distance” gives a linear boundary.

<Hint>Use the midpoint and the vector between the centroids.</Hint>

<Solution>
$\bw = \bp - \bn = (4, 2)$, $t = (\|\bp\|^2 - \|\bn\|^2)/2 = (13 - 1)/2 = 6$. Boundary $4x_1 + 2x_2 = 6$, i.e. $2x_1 + x_2 = 3$. Midpoint $(1, 1)$ scores 6 — on the boundary. Query $(1, 2)$ scores 8 > 6: positive; its squared distances are 4 (to p) and 8 (to n), agreeing.

In general, expand $\|\bx - \bp\|^2 = \|\bx - \bn\|^2$, cancel $\bx\T\bx$, and divide by 2 to get $\bx\T(\bp - \bn) = (\|\bp\|^2 - \|\bn\|^2)/2$.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="A3" title="A sigmoid is not a label" lesson="sigmoid">

A logistic model has score $z = -\log 3 + x\log 3$. Find its probabilities at $x = 0, 1, 2$ and the 0.5-threshold labels (ties → class 1). Derive the input boundary for probability threshold 0.75. Would doubling the coefficients preserve the probabilities as well as the 0.5 boundary?

<Hint>exp(log 3) = 3.</Hint>

<Solution>
Scores $-\log 3, 0, \log 3$ give probabilities $1/4, 1/2, 3/4$ and labels 0, 1, 1. For τ = 0.75 we need $z \ge \log(0.75/0.25) = \log 3$, i.e. $x \ge 2$. Doubling the coefficients keeps the sign of the score, so the 0.5 boundary (x = 1) is unchanged, but the probabilities change away from z = 0: at x = 0 it becomes $\sigma(-2\log 3) = 1/(1 + 9) = 1/10$ instead of 1/4.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="A4" title="Likelihood to gradient" lesson="logfit">

Starting with $p_j = \sigma(\bxt_j\T\beta)$ and binary labels $y_j$, derive the average negative log-likelihood and its gradient. State where independence enters. Explain why setting this gradient to zero does not give Week 1’s normal-equation solution.

<Hint>Differentiate one observation with respect to its score before summing.</Hint>

<Solution>
Independence gives $\Lik = \prod_j p_j^{y_j}(1 - p_j)^{1 - y_j}$; the negative average log is

$$
J = -\frac1m\sum_j\big[y_j\log p_j + (1 - y_j)\log(1 - p_j)\big].
$$

With $\sigma' = \sigma(1 - \sigma)$, the factor $p_j(1 - p_j)$ cancels the denominators, leaving score derivative $p_j - y_j$. Hence $\nabla J = \frac1m\sum_j(p_j - y_j)\bxt_j = \frac1m X\T(\bp - \by)$. Because $\bp = \sigma(X\beta)$ entry by entry, the zero-gradient condition is nonlinear in β — it is not $X\T X\beta = X\T\by$.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="A5" title="Metric or merely a formula?" lesson="metric">

State the metric axioms. Use $(0,0), (1,0), (1,1)$ to test the triangle inequality for $p = \tfrac12$. Then explain why

$$
d(\bx, \bz) = \sqrt{(x_1 - z_1)^2 + 0\cdot(x_2 - z_2)^2}
$$

is a pseudometric on ℝ² but not a metric.

<Hint>One counterexample disproves an axiom.</Hint>

<Solution>
A metric is non-negative, zero only for identical points, symmetric, and satisfies the triangle inequality. For p = ½ the direct distance from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$ is $(1 + 1)^2 = 4$ while the legs via $(1,0)$ are 1 and 1: violated. The weighted expression is $|x_1 - z_1|$; distinct points such as $(1, 0), (1, 8)$ have distance 0, while symmetry and the triangle inequality still hold — a pseudometric.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="A6" title="Mean versus medoid" lesson="exemplar">

For points 1, 4, 10 on the real line, calculate the mean and a medoid (absolute distance). Show why the mean minimises the sum of squared distances by expanding around it. Why can the mean and the medoid differ without either being wrong?

<Hint>The deviations from the mean sum to zero.</Hint>

<Solution>
Mean 5. Total distances at candidates 1, 4, 10 are 12, 9, 15, so the medoid is 4. Expanding about the mean,

$$
\sum_j(x_j - c)^2 = \sum_j(x_j - 5)^2 + 3(c - 5)^2,
$$

because $\sum_j(x_j - 5) = 0$; the sum is uniquely minimised at $c = 5$. The two questions differ in the objective (squared vs absolute distance) and in the allowed centres (any point vs an observed point).
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="B1" title="One logistic update" lesson="logfit">

Data $(x, y) = (0, 1), (2, 0)$, start at $\beta = (0, 0)\T$, learning rate 0.2, average log loss. Compute the gradient, the simultaneous update, and the two new probabilities.

<Solution>
$$
X = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 1 & 2\end{pmatrix}, \quad \bp - \by = \begin{pmatrix}-0.5\\ 0.5\end{pmatrix}, \quad \nabla J = \tfrac12X\T(\bp - \by) = \begin{pmatrix}0\\ 0.5\end{pmatrix}.
$$

$\beta_{\text{new}} = (0, 0) - 0.2(0, 0.5) = (0, -0.1)$. Probabilities: $\sigma(0) = 0.5$ and $\sigma(-0.2) \approx 0.4502$ — the negative observation’s estimated positive probability fell. The average loss drops from 0.6931 to about 0.6456.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="B2" title="A confusion matrix" lesson="metrics">

On 100 cases, $\TP = 18$, $\FN = 12$, $\FP = 6$, $\TN = 64$. Compute accuracy, precision, recall, F1 and FPR, and name the population each denominator counts.

<Solution>
30 actual positives, 70 actual negatives, 24 predicted positives.

$$
\Acc = 0.82, \quad \Prec = \tfrac{18}{24} = 0.75, \quad \Rec = \tfrac{18}{30} = 0.60, \quad \Fone = \tfrac{36}{54} = \tfrac23, \quad \FPR = \tfrac{6}{70} \approx 0.0857.
$$

Accuracy: all cases; precision: predicted positives; recall: actual positives; FPR: actual negatives. F1 combines TP, FP, FN.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="B3" title="Construct an ROC curve" lesson="roc">

Scores $(0.9, 0.7, 0.6, 0.2)$ with labels $(1, 0, 1, 0)$. Sweep the threshold from above 0.9 to below 0.2 (positive at or above it). List the ROC points and compute AUC. What are accuracy and recall at threshold 0.65?

<Solution>
Points $(0,0), (0,\tfrac12), (\tfrac12,\tfrac12), (\tfrac12,1), (1,1)$; AUC $= \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cdot1 = \tfrac34$. At 0.65 the first two are predicted positive: TP = FP = FN = TN = 1, so accuracy ½ and recall ½. AUC and the accuracy at one threshold answer different questions.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="B4" title="Uniform and weighted neighbours" lesson="weighted">

Query $x = 0$ against $(0.5, 1), (1, 0), (2, 0), (4, 1), (6, 0)$. Compute the 1-NN, unweighted 3-NN and inverse-square weighted 3-NN predictions. Why does using all observations with uniform votes give a constant predictor, while query-dependent weights need not?

<Solution>
1-NN: nearest is 0.5 → 1. Uniform 3-NN: labels 1, 0, 0 → 0. Weights $4, 1, \tfrac14$: class 1 gets 4, class 0 gets 1.25 → 1. With $k = m$ uniform counts are identical for every query; distance weights change with the query and can change the winner.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="B5" title="Distances" lesson="distance">

For $\bx = (1, 2, 3, 4)$, $\bz = (3, 5, 9, 4)$, compute the Manhattan, Euclidean, Chebyshev and mismatch distances. Can squared Euclidean distance replace Euclidean distance both for ranking neighbours and in the inverse-square weight without adjustment?

<Solution>
Differences $(2, 3, 6, 0)$: $d_1 = 11$, $d_2 = \sqrt{49} = 7$, $d_\infty = 6$, $d_0 = 3$. For ranking, yes — the order is preserved. For weighting, if $s = d_2^2$ then the inverse-square weight is $1/s$, not $1/s^2$ (which would be inverse fourth power).
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="B6" title="Leave one out" lesson="lazyeval">

For $(x, y) = (0,0), (2,0), (3,1), (7,1), (8,1)$, compute the 1-NN training error with self-matches and the 1-NN LOOCV error. Is $k = 5$ a valid candidate inside this LOOCV?

<Solution>
Training error 0. Leaving each out, the nearest remaining inputs are 2, 3, 2, 8, 7, predicting 0, 1, 0, 1, 1 for true 0, 0, 1, 1, 1: two errors, LOOCV error $2/5$. Each fit has only 4 reference points, so $k = 5$ is not valid.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Group id="C" title="C · Diagnose each statement" intro="True or false — and give the mechanism or a counterexample, not just a verdict.">

<Problem id="C1" title="Diagnose the claim" lesson="logfit">

“A convex logistic loss always has exactly one finite coefficient vector that minimises it.”

<Solution>
**False.** Convexity makes any stationary point a global minimiser but guarantees neither existence nor uniqueness. Redundant columns give non-unique minimisers; separable data drive the unregularised optimum to unbounded coefficients (e.g. $\log(1 + e^{-b}) \to 0$ as $b \to \infty$).
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="C2" title="Diagnose the claim" lesson="roc">

“FPR is one minus precision, because both concern incorrect positive predictions.”

<Solution>
**False.** $\FPR = \FP/(\FP + \TN)$ divides by actual negatives; $1 - \Prec = \FP/(\TP + \FP)$ divides by predicted positives. Same numerator, different conditional questions.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="C3" title="Diagnose the claim" lesson="cv">

“Before cross-validation I computed all scaling and imputation values on the complete dataset. No labels were used, so this cannot leak information.”

<Solution>
**False.** Fitted preprocessing can carry information from held-out inputs without labels. Fit scaling and imputation inside each training fold, then apply them to its validation fold.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="C4" title="Diagnose the claim" lesson="knn">

“Choosing an odd k removes every possible tie in k-NN.”

<Solution>
**False.** It prevents a tied two-class vote once exactly k neighbours are chosen. It does not resolve equal distances at the neighbourhood boundary, multi-class voting ties, or duplicate inputs with conflicting labels.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="C5" title="Diagnose the claim" lesson="datatypes">

“The labels red = 1, green = 2, blue = 3 establish meaningful Euclidean distances between colours.”

<Solution>
**False.** The codes are arbitrary: relabelling the colours would change the distances without changing the categories. Use matching distance or indicator features.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="C6" title="Diagnose the claim" lesson="curse">

“More than 20 features necessarily makes k-NN unusable, whereas normalising any smaller number guarantees meaningful neighbours.”

<Solution>
**False.** The dimension counts are practical warnings, not thresholds. Relevance, structure, sample size and the distance matter; normalisation fixes units, not irrelevance, noise or lack of representative data.
</Solution>

</Problem>

</Group>

<Problem id="D1" title="Design an evaluation" lesson="roc">

A screening test must flag a condition present in about 2% of 5,000 patients. You will fit a logistic model and must choose a threshold. Describe the data split, which metrics you would report and why, how you would choose the threshold, and what accuracy alone would hide.

<Solution>
Reserve a stratified test set first. On the development data, use (stratified) K-fold CV: fit any scaling/imputation and the logistic model inside each training fold. Report recall (missed cases are costly), precision (how many alarms are real), and the ROC curve / AUC to compare models across thresholds. Choose τ on validation predictions to meet a recall target or a cost trade-off — not 0.5 by default, and not on the test set. Then refit and report the confusion matrix at the chosen τ on the test set once. Accuracy alone would be about 0.98 for “everyone is healthy”, hiding a recall of 0.
</Solution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="D2" title="Choose a classifier" lesson="exemplar">

You have 2,000 labelled examples with 6 numeric features on very different scales. One class forms two separate clusters. Compare nearest centroid, logistic regression on the raw features, and k-NN as first choices. What preprocessing and evaluation would each need?

<Solution>
| Model | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| Nearest centroid | poor: one mean for the two-cluster class lands between the clusters |
| Logistic regression | linear boundary in the raw features cannot separate a class with clusters on both sides; feature transformations would be needed |
| k-NN | natural first choice: local rule handles multimodal classes; d = 6 and m = 2,000 are comfortable |

k-NN needs feature scaling (fitted on training folds only), a check for irrelevant features, and k chosen by cross-validation; report the chosen procedure once on a held-out test set.
</Solution>

</Problem>
