## Deciding from the prior alone

Back to the fish. From past catches, 30% are salmon and 70% sea bass. With no measurement at all, the best guess is always “sea bass”. This *decision rule based on the prior* is right 70% of the time — and never predicts salmon. Measurements should let us do better.

Suppose we also know how lengths are distributed *within* each species — the class-conditional probabilities:

<Table
          head={['P(x | c)', 'salmon', 'sea bass']}
          rows={[
            ['length > 100 cm', '0.5', '0.3'],
            ['50 cm < length < 100 cm', '0.4', '0.5'],
            ['length < 50 cm', '0.1', '0.2'],
          ]}
        />

Each column sums to 1: it is a distribution over lengths *for one class*. What we want is the reverse, $P(c \mid x)$: given this length, which species?

## Bayes’ theorem

<Steps>
Bayes’ theorem is not a new axiom — it falls out of describing one joint event in two orders.

<Step title="Conditioning restricts attention">
$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \wedge B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B) > 0 \quad\text{}.
$$
</Step>

<Step title="The product rule, both ways round">
$$
P(A \wedge B) = P(A \mid B)P(B) = P(B \mid A)P(A).
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Equate and divide">
$$
\boxed{\;P(c \mid x) = \frac{P(x \mid c)\,P(c)}{P(x)}\;}
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Total probability gives the denominator">
$$
P(x) = \sum_{c'} P(x \mid c')\,P(c')
$$

valid because the classes are mutually exclusive and exhaustive. It is exactly what makes the posteriors sum to 1.
</Step>
</Steps>

| Quantity | Name | Question it answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $P(c)$ | prior | how common is the class before seeing x? |
| $P(x \mid c)$ | likelihood (class-conditional) | if the class were c, how plausible is x? |
| $P(x)$ | evidence (marginal) | how plausible is x across all classes? |
| $P(c \mid x)$ | posterior | after seeing x, how plausible is c? |

The same formula is often written with a hypothesis $h$ and training data $D$ in place of a class and an observation: $P(h \mid D) = P(D \mid h)P(h)/P(D)$ — lesson 3 uses that form.

<Callout kind="warning">
$P(x \mid c)$ and $P(c \mid x)$ answer different questions; never swap them by intuition. And the sum rule is $P(A \vee B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \wedge B)$ — plain addition only works for disjoint events.
</Callout>

## Worked example: a 70 cm fish

<Callout kind="example">
70 cm falls in the 50–100 cm bin. Multiply likelihood by prior:

| class | prior | likelihood | product | posterior |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| salmon | 0.3 | 0.4 | 0.12 | $0.12/0.47 \approx 0.2553$ |
| sea bass | 0.7 | 0.5 | 0.35 | $0.35/0.47 \approx 0.7447$ |

The evidence is $0.12 + 0.35 = 0.47$; predict sea bass. To *choose* a class you can compare 0.12 with 0.35 directly — dividing both by the same positive number cannot change their order. Note we are modelling a length *bin*, not one exact continuous length.
</Callout>

3. Choose “\< 50 cm”: the likelihoods favour sea bass and so does the prior. Evidence and prior agree.

<Callout kind="note" title="Scores versus probabilities">
$s_c = P(x \mid c)P(c)$ is an unnormalised score (for discrete $x$ it is the joint probability $P(x, c)$). The posterior is $s_c / \sum_{c'} s_{c'}$. Drop the denominator for an argmax, never when a probability is asked for.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="key">
Bayes’ theorem $P(c \mid x) = P(x \mid c)P(c)/\sum_{c'}P(x \mid c')P(c')$ reverses a conditional: it combines how plausible the observation is under each class with how common each class is. The evidence normalises; for a decision, compare the scores $P(x \mid c)P(c)$.
</Callout>
