## The most probable model is not the question we care about

A hypothesis can be a collection of predictions, or a function mapping inputs to predictions. So far we sought $h_{\text{MAP}}$. But given a new instance $x$, what we really want is its most probable *classification* — and $h_{\text{MAP}}(x)$ is not necessarily that, especially when several hypotheses support the same class.

| hypothesis | $P(h \mid D)$ | prediction at x |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $h_1$ | 0.4 | + |
| $h_2$ | 0.3 | − |
| $h_3$ | 0.3 | − |

The MAP hypothesis $h_1$ predicts +. But the posterior weight supporting − is $0.3 + 0.3 = 0.6$. The most probable class is −.

## The Bayes optimal classifier

Instead of selecting one hypothesis, average over all of them, weighted by their posteriors:

$$
P(v_j \mid D) = \sum_{h_i \in H} P(v_j \mid h_i)\,P(h_i \mid D), \qquad \hat v = \argmax_{v_j \in V}\sum_{h_i\in H} P(v_j \mid h_i)\,P(h_i \mid D).
$$

<Steps>
Apply it to the example. Each deterministic hypothesis gives probability 1 to its own prediction.

<Step title="Write each hypothesis’s vote">
$$
P(+ \mid h_1) = 1,\; P(- \mid h_1) = 0; \qquad P(+ \mid h_{2,3}) = 0,\; P(- \mid h_{2,3}) = 1.
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Weight by the posterior and add">
$$
\sum_i P(+ \mid h_i)P(h_i \mid D) = 0.4, \qquad \sum_i P(- \mid h_i)P(h_i \mid D) = 0.3 + 0.3 = 0.6.
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Choose the largest">
The Bayes optimal classification is −, although the single most probable hypothesis says +.
</Step>
</Steps>

It is a *weighted* average, not an unweighted majority vote; for probabilistic hypotheses use their actual class probabilities $P(v \mid x, h)$.

<Callout kind="theorem" title="Why “optimal”">
Under zero-one loss, predicting $y$ is wrong with probability $1 - P(y \mid x, D)$, so choosing the largest posterior predictive probability minimises the error. No other method using the same hypothesis space and the same prior knowledge does better *on average*. Optimality is relative to that hypothesis space, prior and probability model — not a promise of perfect real-world labels.
</Callout>

Why do we need anything else? The Bayes rule depends on unknown quantities — the true posteriors — which must be approximated from data; and summing over every hypothesis can be very expensive.

## The Gibbs classifier: sample instead of averaging

Gibbs algorithm:

1. Choose one hypothesis at random, according to $P(h \mid D)$.
2. Use it to classify the new instance.

In the example, Gibbs predicts + with probability 0.4 and − with probability 0.6 — it does not always pick the heavier side. Surprisingly, if target concepts are drawn at random from $H$ according to the prior, then

$$
\E[\text{error}_{\text{Gibbs}}] \le 2\,\E[\text{error}_{\text{Bayes optimal}}].
$$

<p className="small muted">This is a bound on expected error under that assumption, not a guarantee for every dataset or prediction. Gibbs avoids evaluating every hypothesis, though sampling from the posterior can itself be hard.</p>

<Callout kind="key">
The most probable hypothesis need not make the most probable prediction. The Bayes optimal classifier predicts $\argmax_v\sum_h P(v \mid h)P(h \mid D)$, which minimises expected error for the given hypothesis space and prior. Gibbs samples one hypothesis from the posterior: cheaper, with expected error at most twice the Bayes optimal under the stated assumptions.
</Callout>
