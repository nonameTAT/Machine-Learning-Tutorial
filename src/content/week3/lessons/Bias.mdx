## The observations do not determine the unseen cases

“All models are wrong, but some models are useful” (Box & Draper). Here is why a learner cannot avoid being “wrong” in some respect. Suppose the only observations are $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$. Both

$$
f(x) = x \qquad\text{and}\qquad g(x) = x + 10x(x - 1)
$$

fit them perfectly, yet at $x = 2$ they predict 2 and 22. Training agreement alone cannot tell us which extrapolation is sensible. To say anything about unseen inputs a learner must prefer some patterns over others.

## Inductive bias

**Inductive bias** is the set of assumptions a model or learning algorithm uses to generalise from the training data to new, unseen data. It includes the hypothesis family, what counts as “similar”, and preferences among competing fits. A good inductive bias — one that matches the real structure of the problem — can dramatically reduce how much data is needed. For complex models it is often hard to state.

| Method | Central inductive bias |
| --- | --- |
| Linear regression | the target is well approximated by a linear function of the chosen features; squared errors decide the fit |
| k-nearest neighbours | nearby points (under the chosen distance and scaling) tend to have similar labels — local similarity |
| Naive Bayes (this week) | features are mutually independent once the class is known, each with a chosen distribution |

<p className="small muted">k-NN’s bias is sometimes described as “the target is a complex non-linear function”. k-NN can produce non-linear boundaries, but its central assumption is local similarity.</p>

<Callout kind="warning" title="Two meanings of “bias”">
*Inductive* bias means assumptions. *Statistical* bias (Week 1’s bias–variance decomposition) means systematic error in an estimator. They are related — strong assumptions such as linearity tend to give high bias and low variance — but a strong inductive bias that *matches* the problem need not create large statistical bias. “Stronger assumptions always mean higher error” is false.
</Callout>

## A probabilistic framework

We want a framework that represents the inductive bias explicitly and declaratively, and quantifies uncertainty. Probability does this. For example, with two Boolean features of an email and the class $Y \in \{\text{spam}, \text{ham}\}$:

A **decision rule** turns this posterior into a prediction — e.g. predict spam when $P(\text{spam} \mid x) > 0.5$. Bayesian methods play two roles: practical algorithms (Naive Bayes, Bayesian networks) that combine prior knowledge with data, and a conceptual “gold standard” for judging other learners. ## Discriminative and generative models

A **discriminative** probabilistic model learns $P(y \mid x)$ directly — logistic regression from Week 2. A **generative** model learns $P(y)$ and $P(x \mid y)$, hence the joint $P(x, y) = P(x \mid y)P(y)$, and obtains the posterior from it:

$$
P(y \mid x) = \frac{P(x, y)}{\sum_{c} P(x, Y = c)}.
$$

It is called generative because you could *generate* labelled data: sample a class from $P(y)$, then features from $P(x \mid y)$. Naive Bayes is generative. The distinction concerns *what is modelled*, not whether the boundary looks straight.

<Callout kind="key">
Finite data are consistent with many hypotheses, so every learner needs an inductive bias — assumptions that pick out plausible generalisations. Linear regression assumes linearity, k-NN assumes local similarity, Naive Bayes assumes conditional independence. The probabilistic framework makes such assumptions explicit and quantifies uncertainty; generative models learn $P(x \mid y)P(y)$ and use Bayes’ rule.
</Callout>
