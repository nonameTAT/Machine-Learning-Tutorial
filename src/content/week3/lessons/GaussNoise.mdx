## Where do P(D | h) and P(h) come from?

To use Bayes’ theorem we need the likelihood and the prior. There are two routes: estimate them *empirically* from counts in the data (Naive Bayes, lessons 9–11), or assume a *parametric model* and estimate its parameters from the data. This lesson takes the second route for regression — and recovers Week 1’s squared error.

## Learning a real-valued function

Training examples $(x_i, y_i)$ are noisy values of a target function:

$$
y_i = f(x_i) + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i \sim \N(0, \sigma^2) \text{ independently for each } i.
$$

Under a hypothesis $h$, each $y_i$ is Gaussian around $h(x_i)$, and its density decreases exponentially with the *squared* distance from that mean:

$$
p(y_i \mid x_i, h) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(y_i - h(x_i))^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big).
$$

<Steps>
Treat the inputs and σ² as fixed. The ML hypothesis maximises the probability of the observed targets.

<Step title="Multiply (independence across observations)">
$$
h_{\text{ML}} = \argmax_{h\in H} P(D \mid h) = \argmax_{h\in H}\prod_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}e^{-\frac{(y_i - h(x_i))^2}{2\sigma^2}}.
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Take the log (strictly increasing, keeps the maximiser)">
$$
\log P(D \mid h) = -\frac n2\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - h(x_i))^2.
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Drop what cannot affect the comparison">
The first term does not involve $h$. The factor $1/(2\sigma^2)$ is positive and fixed. Maximising a negative multiple of the squared error is minimising the squared error.
</Step>

<Step title="Result">
$$
\boxed{\;h_{\text{ML}} = \argmin_{h\in H}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - h(x_i))^2\;}
$$

Dividing by $n$ gives the MSE — same minimiser. This is Week 1’s lesson 7, now as an instance of Bayesian learning.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Callout kind="note" title="What the result does — and does not — say">
Independent, zero-mean Gaussian noise with a common variance makes least squares the maximum-likelihood procedure. It does not say every dataset has Gaussian noise, nor that a small training error guarantees generalisation.
</Callout>

## The noise model decides which mistakes matter

<Callout kind="example" title="Worked example">
Two candidate models have residuals $(1, 1)$ and $(0, 2)$. Both have total absolute error 2, but squared errors 2 and 4. Under the same Gaussian noise the first model is more likely: Gaussian noise considers one large error much less plausible than two moderate ones.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="warning" title="Two different independence assumptions">
This derivation assumes independence *across training observations*. Naive Bayes (lesson 9) assumes independence *across features given the class*. They act on different parts of the model.

<Callout kind="key">
If $y_i = f(x_i) + \varepsilon_i$ with independent $\varepsilon_i \sim \N(0, \sigma^2)$, the log-likelihood is a constant minus $\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(y_i - h(x_i))^2$, so the ML hypothesis minimises squared error. The loss is the negative log of the noise density: change the noise model and you change which mistakes matter.
</Callout>
