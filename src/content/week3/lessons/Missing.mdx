## Missing is not the same as zero

“The word does not occur” is a *known* Bernoulli value, 0 — it contributes an absence factor. “We have not checked whether it occurs” is missing information, which must account for both possibilities. Week 2’s warning applies here too: never quietly replace a missing value with 0.

A very basic approach for Naive Bayes:

- **Training:** an instance with a missing value is not included in the frequency counts for that attribute’s value–class combinations. Its other attributes, and its class label for the prior, are still used.
- **Classification:** the attribute is omitted from the calculation.

With $N_{jc}^{\text{obs}}$ observed values of feature $j$ in class $c$, smoothing becomes

$$
\hat P(X_j = v \mid c) = \frac{N_{j,v,c} + \alpha}{N_{jc}^{\text{obs}} + \alpha K_j}.
$$

<p className="small muted">This assumes the observed cases are suitable for estimating that feature’s distribution (compare Week 2, lesson 10).</p>

## Why omitting the factor is exactly right for Naive Bayes

<Steps>
Let $o$ be the observed features and $m$ one missing categorical feature. The right quantity is the probability of what we did observe.

<Step title="Marginalise over the unknown value">
$$
P(o \mid c) = \sum_{m} P(o, m \mid c).
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Apply the Naive Bayes factorisation">
$$
\sum_m P(o, m \mid c) = \sum_m P(o \mid c)\,P(m \mid c) = P(o \mid c)\sum_m P(m \mid c).
$$
</Step>

<Step title="The missing feature’s probabilities sum to 1">
So $P(o \mid c)$ is just the product of the *observed* factors: multiply only those. (For a continuous missing feature, its density integrates to 1.)
</Step>
</Steps>

## Can we do better? A general posterior table

Return to the full posterior table from lesson 1. We skimmed an email and saw “lottery”, but not whether it contains “Viagra”. Rows (0, 1) and (1, 1) disagree:

<Table
          head={['Viagra', 'lottery', 'P(spam | ·)', 'decision']}
          rows={[
            ['0', '1', '0.65', 'spam'],
            ['1', '1', '0.40', 'ham'],
          ]}
        />

By total probability, average the rows using the probability of Viagra *among emails with lottery = 1*:

$$
P(\text{spam} \mid L = 1) = \sum_{v\in\{0,1\}} P(\text{spam} \mid V = v, L = 1)\,P(V = v \mid L = 1).
$$

Suppose one in ten emails contains Viagra: $0.65 \times 0.90 + 0.40 \times 0.10 = 0.625$, and $P(\text{ham} \mid L = 1) = 0.375$. Because Viagra is rare, the answer stays close to the (0, 1) row.

<Callout kind="warning" title="A qualification">
That calculation weights by the *marginal* $P(\text{Viagra} = 1) = 0.10$. That is only valid if Viagra and lottery are marginally independent, or if the 0.10 is really $P(\text{Viagra} = 1 \mid \text{lottery} = 1)$. Naive Bayes’ conditional independence given the class does not imply marginal independence. Also, missing is different from an observed but previously unseen category — that is the smoothing problem.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="key">
A missing value is unknown, not zero. In Naive Bayes, marginalising a missing feature multiplies by $\sum_m P(m \mid c) = 1$, so you simply omit its factor; in training, leave it out of that feature’s counts. For a general posterior table, average over the unknown value with weights conditional on what *was* observed.
</Callout>
