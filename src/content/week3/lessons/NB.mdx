## The practical obstacle

Naive Bayes is, along with decision trees, neural networks and nearest neighbour, one of the most practical learning methods. It applies the MAP rule to an instance described by attributes $\langle x_1, \dots, x_d\rangle$:

$$
v_{\text{MAP}} = \argmax_{v_j\in V} P(v_j \mid x_1, \dots, x_d) = \argmax_{v_j\in V} P(x_1, \dots, x_d \mid v_j)\,P(v_j).
$$

Estimating $P(v_j)$ is easy: count. Estimating $P(x_1, \dots, x_d \mid v_j)$ is not. The number of possible feature combinations explodes, and most of them never appear in the training data, so their probabilities cannot be counted.

## The Naive Bayes assumption

Replace the large joint table with one small distribution per feature and class:

$$
P(x_1, \dots, x_d \mid v_j) = \prod_{i=1}^d P(x_i \mid v_j).
$$

Attributes are assumed statistically independent *given the class*: once the class is known, the value of one attribute tells us nothing about another. This is a **modelling assumption**, not a consequence of Bayes’ theorem.

<Steps>
What does the assumption actually replace? Look at two features.

<Step title="The exact chain rule">
$$
P(x_1, x_2 \mid c) = P(x_1 \mid c)\,P(x_2 \mid x_1, c).
$$
</Step>

<Step title="The assumption">
$$
P(x_2 \mid x_1, c) = P(x_2 \mid c)\;\Longrightarrow\; P(x_1, x_2 \mid c) = P(x_1 \mid c)\,P(x_2 \mid c).
$$

Knowing feature 1 does not change the distribution of feature 2 within the class.
</Step>

<Step title="Many features">
The full product needs *mutual* conditional independence of all features given the class — not merely independence of each pair.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Callout kind="warning" title="Three different “independences”">
- **Conditional** (what NB assumes): independent *within* each class.
- **Marginal**: independent overall. Features can be associated overall while independent within each class — two words both common in spam and rare in ham become associated once the classes are mixed.
- **Independent of the class**: that would mean the feature carries no information about the class at all — the opposite of what we want.
</Callout>

## The classifier, and how to train it

Substitute the assumption into the MAP rule:

$$
\boxed{\;v_{\text{NB}} = \argmax_{v_j\in V}\; \hat P(v_j)\prod_{i} \hat P(x_i \mid v_j)\;}
$$

For fully observed categorical data the estimates are counts:

$$
\hat P(c) = \frac{N_c}{N}, \qquad \hat P(X_j = v \mid c) = \frac{N_{j,v,c}}{N_c},
$$

where $N_{j,v,c}$ counts class-$c$ examples with feature $j$ equal to $v$. Training is counting; prediction is multiplying the relevant factors and comparing the class scores.

<Callout kind="note" title="Where the savings come from">
With $d$ binary features, a full table needs $2^d - 1$ free probabilities per class; Naive Bayes needs $d$. Fewer parameters mean less estimation noise — at the cost of discarding interactions.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="intuition" title="Why it often works anyway">
The assumption is often violated, yet NB works surprisingly well. For classification you do not need the estimated posteriors to be right — only that the correct class gets the largest score. But NB posteriors are often unrealistically close to 0 or 1, and redundant (e.g. identical) attributes cause problems. Lesson 17 returns to this.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="key">
The full likelihood $P(x_1, \dots, x_d \mid c)$ is unlearnable for many features. Naive Bayes assumes the features are mutually independent given the class, so the likelihood factorises: $\hat v = \argmax_c \hat P(c)\prod_j \hat P(x_j \mid c)$, estimated by counting within each class.
</Callout>
