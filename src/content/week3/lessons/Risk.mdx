## Probabilities describe uncertainty; losses describe consequences

So far: decide $h_1$ if $P(h_1 \mid D) > P(h_2 \mid D)$. But a posterior does not by itself say which *action* is best. What if misclassifying a patient who has cancer as “not cancer” costs ten times as much as the reverse mistake?

Let $L(a, c)$ be the loss of taking action $a$ when the truth is $c$. The **conditional risk** — the posterior expected loss — of an action is

$$
R(a \mid x) = \sum_c L(a, c)\,P(c \mid x), \qquad a^*(x) = \argmin_a R(a \mid x).
$$

The optimal Bayesian decision minimises expected loss. When every mistake costs the same, this reduces to MAP (proved below).

## Worked example: the positive test again

| action ↓ / truth → | cancer | not cancer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| predict cancer | 0 | 1 |
| predict not cancer | 10 | 0 |

Rows are actions, columns the unknown truth. For each row, weight each entry by the posterior of its column (from lesson 4, 0.2085 and 0.7915) and sum:

$$
R(\text{cancer} \mid +) = 0 \times 0.2085 + 1 \times 0.7915 = 0.7915, \qquad R(\text{not cancer} \mid +) = 10 \times 0.2085 + 0 = 2.085.
$$

The minimum-risk action is now **predict cancer**, although its posterior is the smaller one. The posterior did not change; the objective did.

<Callout kind="note" title="A shortcut">
You can compare $0.02976$ with $0.0784$ — the risks computed with *unnormalised* scores. That is valid because both actions share the same evidence denominator; the true expected losses are 0.7915 and 2.085.
</Callout>

## Derive the threshold instead of memorising it

<Steps>
Two classes, $p = P(c_1 \mid x)$, zero loss for correct predictions, cost $C_{\text{FP}}$ for predicting $c_1$ wrongly and $C_{\text{FN}}$ for missing $c_1$.

<Step title="Risk of each action">
$$
R(c_1 \mid x) = C_{\text{FP}}(1 - p), \qquad R(c_2 \mid x) = C_{\text{FN}}\,p.
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Choose c₁ when its risk is smaller">
$$
C_{\text{FP}}(1 - p) < C_{\text{FN}}\,p \iff \boxed{\;p > \frac{C_{\text{FP}}}{C_{\text{FP}} + C_{\text{FN}}}\;}
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Apply it">
Here the threshold is $1/(1 + 10) \approx 0.0909$, so a posterior of 0.2085 is enough to act. At equality both actions have the same risk. With equal costs the threshold is ½: MAP.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Steps>
More generally, with any number of classes, zero-one loss gives MAP.

<Step title="Zero-one loss">
$$
L(a, c) = \begin{cases} 0 & a = c \\ 1 & a \ne c \end{cases} \;\Rightarrow\; R(a \mid x) = \sum_{c \ne a} P(c \mid x) = 1 - P(a \mid x).
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Minimise">
Minimising $1 - P(a \mid x)$ is maximising $P(a \mid x)$: the MAP rule is the special case of equal costs.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Lab title="Two actions, two risk lines" purpose="Each line is the expected loss of one action as the posterior varies. The cheaper action is the lower line; they cross at the threshold.">

</Lab>

<TryThis>
1. Default: the posterior 0.2085 lies right of the threshold 1/11, inside the “predict present” region, though MAP says absent.
2. Set both costs equal: the lines cross at ½ and minimum risk agrees with MAP everywhere.
3. Make false positives expensive (C_FP = 10, C_FN = 1). Now the posterior must exceed 10/11 before you act.
</TryThis>

In summary, the Bayesian framework lets losses enter the decision rule: minimise the posterior expected loss. Week 2’s choice of an ROC operating threshold is the same idea in practice.

<Callout kind="key">
A decision needs both a posterior and a loss. Minimise the conditional risk $R(a \mid x) = \sum_c L(a, c)P(c \mid x)$. For two classes, act on $c_1$ when $p > C_{\text{FP}}/(C_{\text{FP}} + C_{\text{FN}})$; with zero-one loss this is the MAP rule.
</Callout>
