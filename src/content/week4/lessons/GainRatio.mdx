## The memorisation trap

Give every example a unique identifier and offer it as an attribute. Splitting on the ID puts each example in its own branch; every branch is pure; so the gain equals the whole parent entropy — the largest gain possible. Yet the test is useless: a new example has a new ID, which no branch has seen, and the tree has learned no relationship between inputs and labels.

The ID is the extreme case of a general tendency. The more values an attribute has, the smaller its branches, and small branches are often pure by chance. Information gain rewards that fine division even when it reflects nothing stable. So we want to account for *how finely* a test divides the examples, separately from how well it separates the labels.

## Split entropy and gain ratio

Let $q_v = |S_v| / |S|$ be the probability of taking branch $v$. The **split entropy** (also called split information) is the entropy of *which branch* an example takes:

$$
\operatorname{SplitH}(S, A) = -\sum_{v \in \mathcal V_A} q_v \log_2 q_v, \qquad \operatorname{GR}(S, A) = \frac{\operatorname{IG}(S, A)}{\operatorname{SplitH}(S, A)}.
$$

Both are entropies, but of different distributions: the gain concerns *labels*, the denominator only *branch membership*. A test with $b$ equally sized branches has split entropy $\log_2 b$, so gain ratio divides by a penalty that grows with the number of branches.

## A surprise on PlayTennis

Now let the row identifier Day compete at the root of PlayTennis. Its gain is the full $0.940$; its split entropy is $\log_2 14 = 3.807$; so its gain ratio is $0.247$. Outlook’s gain ratio is only $0.2467 / 1.5774 = 0.156$.

<TryThis>
1. At the root, compare the gain and gain-ratio columns. Day wins under both.

3. Turn Day off: gain ratio now picks Outlook, as gain did. The fix for identifiers is to remove them, not to hope a score will reject them.
</TryThis>

<Callout kind="warning" title="No training-set score can see that an ID won’t generalise">
Every criterion computed from training counts sees fourteen perfectly pure branches. Gain ratio weakens the preference for many values; it does not eliminate it. Identifiers, timestamps used as categories and similar fields must be excluded by the analyst, and validation (lesson 12) remains the real test.
</Callout>

## Boundaries and limits

- If all examples take one branch, split entropy and gain are both 0: the ratio is undefined. Treat such a test as unavailable.
- A very unbalanced split has a tiny split entropy, which can make a weak test look attractive (the “tiny pure branch” above).
- Gain ratio corrects the preference for many branches; it does not guarantee good generalisation or a globally optimal tree.

<Callout kind="note" title="Three quantities to keep apart">
**Parent entropy**: how mixed the labels are. **Information gain**: how much the test reduces that mixing. **Split entropy**: how finely the test divides the examples, regardless of their labels.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="key">
Information gain favours tests with many small branches, which can be pure by accident. Gain ratio divides the gain by the split entropy — the entropy of branch sizes — to penalise fine partitions. It reduces the bias but cannot remove it (Day still wins on PlayTennis), can be inflated by very unbalanced splits, and is undefined when a test does not split at all.
</Callout>
