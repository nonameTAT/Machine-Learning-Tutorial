## Many trees fit; the learner must prefer one

Week 3 opened with a fact that applies here with full force: the training data never determine the predictions for unseen inputs. Many different trees classify the 14 PlayTennis days perfectly and disagree about other days. Whatever makes a learner pick one of them is its **inductive bias**. For trees it helps to separate two kinds.

|  | Restriction bias | Preference bias |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | limits on which hypotheses can be expressed at all | an ordering of the hypotheses that can be expressed |
| ID3 on categorical attributes | trees over the given attributes — a complete class, so no function of the attributes is ruled out (lesson 2) | shorter trees, with high-gain tests near the root |
| Trees with numeric thresholds | axis-parallel regions only (lesson 1) | the same greedy preference, plus any pruning rule |

ID3’s preference is a version of Occam’s razor — prefer simple explanations — implemented by a search procedure rather than stated as a score. It tends to produce short trees; it does not guarantee the shortest one.

## What “greedy” means

At each node ID3 takes the test with the largest *immediate* gain, commits to it, and never revisits the decision. It is hill-climbing through the space of trees, from simple to complex, with no backtracking. That is fast — and since finding a smallest consistent tree is NP-hard in general, some such compromise is unavoidable. The price is that a locally best test need not begin the best tree. Lesson 6 showed a mild version (a poor root on PlayTennis costs three extra leaves); XOR shows the extreme.

Two further properties of this search are worth knowing. ID3 outputs a *single* tree: it cannot say how many other trees fit the data equally well. And every decision uses all the training examples at the node, as counts, so one mislabelled example shifts a gain only a little — the search is less sensitive to individual errors than a method that reacts to one example at a time.

## XOR: zero gain, then a perfect split

Four equally weighted examples, $Y = A \oplus B$:

| A | B | Y |
| --- | --- | --- |
| f | f | False |
| f | t | True |
| t | f | True |
| t | t | False |

<Steps>
<Step title="Root entropy">
Two True, two False: $H(S) = 1$.
</Step>

<Step title="Gain of A at the root">
$$
\text{A = t: (1 True, 1 False)},\quad \text{A = f: (1 True, 1 False)} \;\Rightarrow\; \operatorname{IG}(S, A) = 1 - \big(\tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac12 \cdot 1\big) = 0.
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Gain of B at the root">
By symmetry, also 0. On its own, neither input says anything about the label.
</Step>

<Step title="Gain of B inside A = t">
Now B = f gives True and B = t gives False: both children pure, local entropy 1, so the gain of B here is 1.
</Step>
</Steps>

The information is in the **interaction**: B’s relationship with Y depends on A. A first step with no immediate benefit makes a perfect second step possible.

<Callout kind="warning" title="Representation succeeded; the stopping rule failed">
A depth-2 tree represents XOR exactly. A procedure that stops whenever every immediate gain is zero cannot discover it. That failure belongs to the extra stopping rule, not to the tree representation — one reason to grow first and prune afterwards (lesson 12).
</Callout>

## XOR in the plane

With numeric features the same interaction appears as a checkerboard: class 1 in two opposite quadrants. Now the root gains are not exactly zero — sampling noise makes some threshold slightly better than the others — so the greedy root split is chosen by noise.

<Callout kind="key">
A learner needs a preference among the many trees that fit the data. ID3’s is a preference bias for short trees with high-gain tests near the root, implemented greedily: best immediate test, no backtracking. XOR shows the cost — every single test has zero gain, yet two tests together are perfect — so “stop when gain is zero” is an extra rule that can fail, and interactions are better found by growing first and pruning later.
</Callout>
