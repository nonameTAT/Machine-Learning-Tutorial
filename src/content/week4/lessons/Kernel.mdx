## From fixed leaves to a moving neighbourhood

A regression tree predicts at $x_0$ by averaging the targets in $x_0$’s leaf. The leaves are fixed in advance, so two nearby queries on either side of a boundary get unrelated predictions, and the fitted function jumps. A **kernel smoother** keeps the idea “predict by averaging nearby targets” but lets the neighbourhood *follow the query*: centre a window on $x_0$, average the targets inside it, possibly giving closer points more say. Week 1’s k-NN regression is a relative: it averages the k nearest targets equally.

For a query $x_0$, give each observation a raw weight from a kernel function $K$, then normalise:

$$
a_i(x_0) = K\Big(\frac{\lVert X_i - x_0\rVert_2}{h}\Big), \qquad w_i(x_0) = \frac{a_i(x_0)}{\sum_{j=1}^n a_j(x_0)}, \qquad \hat f(x_0) = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i(x_0)\,Y_i.
$$

Here $h > 0$ is the **bandwidth**, $\lVert\cdot\rVert_2$ is Euclidean distance (absolute distance in one dimension — the subscript 2 names the norm; nothing is squared or doubled), and $K \ge 0$ turns a scaled distance into a weight. When the denominator is positive the weights sum to 1, so $\hat f(x_0)$ is a weighted average of the contributing $Y_i$ and lies between the smallest and largest of them. If every weight is zero the formula is undefined, and an implementation needs a fallback, such as a wider window.

## Two kernels, and a bandwidth convention to watch

            ['Epanechnikov', <M t="\tfrac34(1 - u^2)\,\mathbb 1\{|u| \le 1\}" />, <M t="|X_i - x_0| < h" />],
          ]}
        />

The boxcar gives every point inside the window the same weight. Epanechnikov gives nearby points more weight and fades to zero at the edge. With these definitions, **the same h means different windows**: the boxcar reaches $h/2$, Epanechnikov reaches $h$. To compare shapes over the same radius $r$, use $h_{\text{box}} = 2r$ and $h_{\text{epan}} = r$. The constant ¾ cancels when the weights are normalised; the factor $1 - u^2$ does not. Forgetting to normalise turns the weighted average into an unscaled sum.

## A three-point calculation

<Steps>
Query $x_0 = 0$; three observations at distances 0, 0.15, 0.30 with responses 1, 0, 1. Compare the kernels over the same radius 0.30.

<Step title="Boxcar with h = 0.60 (radius 0.30)">
All three distances are at most 0.30, so all get weight 1: $\hat f_{\text{box}} = (1 + 0 + 1)/3 = 2/3$.
</Step>

<Step title="Epanechnikov with h = 0.30: scaled distances">
$u = 0,\ 0.5,\ 1$.
</Step>

<Step title="Raw weights">
$$
\tfrac34(1 - 0) = \tfrac34,\qquad \tfrac34(1 - 0.25) = \tfrac{9}{16},\qquad \tfrac34(1 - 1) = 0.
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Normalise">
$$
w = \Big(\tfrac{3/4}{3/4 + 9/16},\ \tfrac{9/16}{3/4 + 9/16},\ 0\Big) = \big(\tfrac47,\ \tfrac37,\ 0\big).
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Average">
$$
\hat f_{\text{epan}} = \tfrac47 \cdot 1 + \tfrac37 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1 = \tfrac47.
$$
</Step>
</Steps>

Same radius, different answers: Epanechnikov gives the middle point less weight than the centre one and the far point none at all — it sits exactly on the boundary — while the boxcar treats all three alike.

## Why “linear” smoother, and how to choose h

Fix the inputs, the kernel and $h$. The weights $w_i(x_0)$ then depend on the $X$’s and the query, never on the responses. So the prediction is *linear in the response vector*: replacing every $Y_i$ by $aY_i + bZ_i$ gives $a\hat f_Y(x_0) + b\hat f_Z(x_0)$. In matrix form the fitted values are $\hat{\mathbf y} = W\mathbf y$ for a smoother matrix $W$. The fitted *curve* need not be a straight line in $x$ at all. (A learned regression tree is not a linear smoother in this sense: which leaf a point shares with which others depends on the responses, through the choice of splits.)

Linearity makes the noise easy to track. With independent noise of variance $\sigma^2$ and fixed weights,

$$
\operatorname{Var}\big(\hat f(x_0)\big) = \sigma^2\sum_i w_i(x_0)^2,
$$

which is $\sigma^2/m$ for a boxcar holding $m$ points. A wider window averages more points and lowers this variance, but averages across genuinely different values of the true function, which adds bias — Week 1’s trade-off once more, now controlled by $h$. Neither the smoothest curve nor the one that passes closest to every point is automatically the best predictor; choose $h$ by validation.

<Callout kind="key">
A kernel smoother predicts at $x_0$ with a normalised weighted average of nearby responses, the neighbourhood moving with the query. The kernel sets the weight shape and, with the bandwidth convention, the window size: boxcar reaches h/2, Epanechnikov h. For fixed weights the estimate is linear in the responses, its noise variance is $\sigma^2\sum_i w_i^2$, and h trades variance (small windows) against bias (large ones).
</Callout>
