## A split can capture a pattern — or an accident

Every split a tree makes is justified by the training examples at one node. Some splits capture a stable pattern; others separate a few examples whose labels happen to be noisy in this particular sample. Both lower the training error, and the training data alone cannot tell them apart.

In fact training error can never go up as the tree grows: splitting a leaf and labelling each child by *its* majority is at least as accurate on those examples as the parent’s single label. So the training error falls steadily — eventually to zero, if no two identical inputs disagree (lesson 11) — whether or not the splits mean anything.

Week 1’s bias–variance picture explains what happens on new data. A deep tree has many small leaves, each estimating its label from a handful of examples; change the sample slightly and many of those leaves change. That is **high variance**. A shallow tree averages over large regions and changes little between samples, but may miss real structure: **high bias**.

## Pre-pruning: constrain growth

The first remedy is to stop growing before the tree memorises the sample. Each common constraint controls something different:

| Constraint | What it limits | scikit-learn parameter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Maximum depth | the number of tests on any path | `max_depth` |
| Maximum number of leaves | the number of regions | `max_leaf_nodes` |
| Minimum samples to split | whether a node has enough examples to attempt a split at all | `min_samples_split` |
| Minimum samples per leaf | whether every child of a proposed split is large enough | `min_samples_leaf` |
| Minimum impurity decrease | whether the best split improves the criterion enough to be worth making | `min_impurity_decrease` |
| Significance test (e.g. χ²) | whether the association between test outcome and label is unlikely to be chance | — |

<Callout kind="warning" title="“Minimum samples to split” is not “minimum samples per leaf”">
With minimum samples to split = 20, a node of 20 examples may be split. With minimum samples per leaf = 5, a proposed 19/1 split of that same node is still rejected, because one child would hold a single example.
</Callout>

Pre-pruning is cheap and fast, but it judges each split in isolation. A split whose value only appears one level deeper — XOR in lesson 9 — is cut off before its usefulness can show. **Post-pruning** grows the full tree and then removes parts that do not earn their keep (lesson 12).

<Callout kind="note" title="Perfect training fit has a precondition">
“An unpruned tree reaches zero training error” holds only when examples with identical inputs have identical labels. Otherwise some errors survive any amount of splitting — lesson 11.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="key">
Training error can only fall as a tree grows, so it cannot tell a real pattern from a memorised accident. Deep trees have small, unstable leaves — high variance; shallow trees may miss structure — high bias. Pre-pruning limits growth with depth, leaf-size, impurity-decrease or significance rules; it is cheap but can stop just before a useful interaction appears.
</Callout>
