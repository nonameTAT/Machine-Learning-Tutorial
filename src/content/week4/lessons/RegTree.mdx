## Keep the partition, change the output

Nothing about partitioning the input space depends on the label being a class. If the target is a number — a price, a temperature — a tree can still split the inputs into regions; each leaf now predicts a *number*, the same for every input in its region. Two questions follow: which number should a leaf predict, and how should splits be chosen? This lesson answers the first.

Why bother, when Week 1 already fits lines? Think of a drug that does almost nothing at low doses, works very well in a middle range and loses effect at high doses. No straight line follows that, but a few thresholds on dosage — each region predicting its own average effectiveness — do. With several inputs, some numeric and some categorical, the same recipe still applies unchanged.

## Under squared error, a leaf predicts the mean

A leaf receives targets $y_1, \dots, y_m$ and must predict one constant $c$ for all of them. With squared error the total loss is

$$
L(c) = \sum_{i=1}^m (y_i - c)^2.
$$

<Steps>
<Step title="Differentiate">
$$
L'(c) = -2\sum_{i=1}^m (y_i - c) = 2m\,(c - \bar y), \qquad \bar y = \tfrac1m \textstyle\sum_i y_i.
$$
</Step>

<Step title="Set to zero">
$L'(c) = 0 \iff c = \bar y$.
</Step>

<Step title="Check it is a minimum">
$L''(c) = 2m > 0$ for a non-empty leaf, so $\bar y$ is the unique minimiser.
</Step>

<Step title="A second proof, without calculus">
Write $y_i - c = (y_i - \bar y) + (\bar y - c)$, square and sum. The cross term is $2(\bar y - c)\sum_i (y_i - \bar y) = 0$, leaving

$$
\sum_i (y_i - c)^2 = \sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2 + m\,(c - \bar y)^2.
$$

The second term is never negative and vanishes only at $c = \bar y$: any other constant adds exactly $m(c - \bar y)^2$.
</Step>
</Steps>

<TryThis>
1. Targets 1, 2, 2: the minimum is at 5/3. Check the decomposition line: the loss at any c is the loss at the mean plus 3(c − 5/3)².
2. Add the outlier (1, 2, 2, 3, 14): the mean is dragged to 4.4, far from most of the targets.
3. Switch to absolute error: the loss becomes piecewise linear and its minimum moves to the median, 2 — much less affected by the outlier (Week 1’s loss functions again).
</TryThis>

At its optimum, the leaf’s average squared error is $\tfrac1m\sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2$: the variance of the leaf’s targets with denominator $m$. The unbiased sample variance divides by $m - 1$; that answers a different question (estimating a population variance) and is not the training objective here.

## The shape of a regression tree

A regression tree with leaf regions $R_1, \dots, R_L$ and leaf means $\bar y_\ell$ computes

$$
\hat f(x) = \sum_{\ell=1}^{L} \bar y_\ell\;\mathbb{1}\{x \in R_\ell\},
$$

a **piecewise-constant** function. The partition is adaptive — steps go where the data need them — but within a leaf the prediction is flat. That suits abrupt changes and produces staircases on smooth trends.

<TryThis>

2. Test MSE is lowest around depth 4 here. By depth 8 the tree has around 70 leaves, chases individual noisy points, and its training MSE falls far below the noise variance σ² = 0.04 — a sure sign of overfitting.
3. Look beyond x = 10. The curve keeps rising, but the tree predicts the constant of its last leaf forever: a constant-leaf tree cannot extrapolate a trend.
4. Raise the minimum leaf size to 10: deep trees can no longer isolate single points, and the test curve flattens.
</TryThis>

<Callout kind="warning" title="Two limitations worth stating in an exam">
Splitting on $x$ does not fit a slope inside a leaf — that needs a model tree (lesson 16). And a distant input still receives the constant of whichever terminal region it falls in, however far from the data it is.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="key">
A regression tree keeps the partition and changes the output: each leaf predicts a constant, and under squared error the best constant is the mean of the leaf’s targets — by calculus or by the identity $\sum(y - c)^2 = \sum(y - \bar y)^2 + m(c - \bar y)^2$. The result is a piecewise-constant function: adaptive, step-shaped, and unable to extrapolate.
</Callout>
